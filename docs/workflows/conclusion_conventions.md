# Conclusion conventions: what a conclusion chapter is for, and how it fails

**Status:** durable. Load this before drafting, scrutinising or cutting chapter 7.
It is the chapter-level yardstick. [`voice.md`](voice.md) §(0) governs the
sentences, [`connective_prose.md`](connective_prose.md) the opener, and
[`threats_terminology.md`](threats_terminology.md) the wording of any limitation
the chapter names. The ch6 yardstick (harvest D, B11) governs the forward
pointers from 6.5. This file does not repeat them.

## (a) What the chapter is for

- **It states the significance of the findings, not a summary of them.**
  "Summaries are not conclusions… conclusions are a statement of the significance
  of what you found out" (Evans, Gruba & Zobel 2014, hereafter EGZ, p. 123).
- **It shows the aim was met, and arises "inescapably" from the discussion**
  (EGZ p. 121). Nothing in it is argued for the first time.
- **It answers two questions:** what the research achieved, and how far it
  "moved professional discussion along" (Dunleavy 2003, p. 207).
- **In CS it consolidates the contribution.** Its main job is "to show how the
  findings and work done contribute to the research field"; the CS community
  "expects thesis writers to emphasize the contribution" (Soler-Monreal 2016,
  pp. 121, 128).
- **It claims the contribution explicitly.** Trafford, Leshem & Bitzer (2014,
  100 PhDs; abstract only) found explicit claims of originality and contribution
  in only 54 % of conclusions.
- **At honours scale it is short.** Cambridge CST Part II: "likely to be very
  short and it may well refer back to the Introduction". UNSW honours: show the
  aims were achieved, "concise and not include new information".

## (b) What the two readers expect

- **The examiner reads it first and last.** Most examiners begin with "the
  abstract, introduction and conclusion" (Mullins & Kiley 2002, p. 376), and it is
  "usually the last substantial text examiners read" before the report (Trafford &
  Leshem, ECER 2009 abstract; Thomson, *patter* 2018, via a fetch summary).
- **The examiner checks it against the introduction.** "Do the conclusions follow
  on from the introduction?" (Mullins & Kiley p. 377); they "check carefully for
  the link" between intentions and their realisation (p. 385). UWA's Discussion
  criterion 1 asks for the results' relation to "the hypotheses and possible
  outcomes that were described in the Introduction".
- **The examiner wants the main conclusion named and bounded.** A poor thesis
  cannot "explain at the end … what had actually been argued" (Mullins & Kiley
  p. 379). Examiners are "vigilant for … conclusions that go beyond what the
  findings can support" (Golding, Sharmini & Lazarovitch 2014, p. 570).
- **The examiner asks "What further research is now warranted?"** (Golding
  p. 570). UWA scores two things under *Discussion*: (5) improvements to the
  recognised shortcomings, and (6) further work, open questions or "loose ends",
  with "ways in which these might be further investigated or resolved".
- **The student reader may read this chapter alone.** It "may be read as a
  stand-alone item" (DoctoralWriting SIG 2016), so it must carry the question,
  the answer, the contribution and its bound without the chapters before it.

## (c) Structure

**The moves, as CS theses use them** (Soler-Monreal 2016, 48 Glasgow CS PhDs,
Table 7; read in full):

| Move | Share | In this dissertation |
|---|---|---|
| M1 Revisiting the study (question, problem, work done) | 100 % | Preamble |
| M2 Consolidating the research space (answers, claims, products) | 100 % | Preamble (answer) and Contributions (warrant) |
| M3 Applications and implications | 50 % | Inside each contribution, as its impact |
| M4 Future work (with limitations, 66.67 %) | 95.83 % | Future work |
| M5 Recapitulating the overall study (overall claims, 52.08 %) | 60.42 % | The close |

1. **Specific to general,** the introduction's funnel run backwards: answer, then
   contributions, then future work, then the close (Soler-Monreal p. 124; Digital
   Health CRC workshop slides).
2. **Two to four generic headings.** Three sections is the commonest shape (25 %);
   headings say *summary*, *contributions*, *future work*, *closing remarks*
   (Soler-Monreal p. 115). 59.5 % open on an unheaded paragraph (p. 115).
3. **Length.** CS conclusions average 4.45 % of the thesis (Soler-Monreal p. 112);
   Cambridge Part II budgets about 500 of 12 000 words (about 4 %); Peyton Jones
   gives a paper half a page in ten. At 15 000 words that is **about 650–900
   words**.
4. **No floats.** The chapter introduces no table or figure; it points to floats
   already argued (EGZ p. 122: "only minimal discussion").

## (d) The parts

### (d1) The preamble: the question answered

**Job.** Return the reader to the research question and give the answer, before
the sections that warrant it.

- **Question, then answer, in one paragraph.** Restate the question once, then
  give the finding as its answer (Soler-Monreal p. 126, worked example T42;
  Thomson 2012, "about three sentences" per question, via a fetch summary).
- **More concrete than the introduction.** Never "simply repeat material from the
  Abstract or Introduction"; "make the original claims more concrete, e.g., by
  referring to quantitative performance results" (Widom, *Tips for writing
  technical papers*). One headline number chapter 6 already gave is allowed.
- **Say which possible outcome happened.** UWA's Introduction criterion asks for
  "the implications of any alternative results"; the preamble names the one the
  results chose.
- **Restate nothing else.** No source asks for the context or the gap to be
  re-argued; chapter 1 carries them.

| Pitfall | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **The abstract again** | Does any sentence repeat the abstract or chapter 1? | DoctoralWriting SIG 2016 ("lazy reiteration"); Widom |
| **The chapter tour** | Does it walk the chapters in order? | EGZ p. 123; Dunleavy p. 207 |
| **No main conclusion** | Can a reader say, in one sentence, what the answer is? | Golding p. 573; Mullins & Kiley p. 379 |
| **Promised one thing, delivered another** | Does the answer answer chapter 1's question as worded? | EGZ p. 64 |

### (d2) Contributions: the promises, delivered

**Job.** Restate each contribution chapter 1 promised as a claim now standing on
its evidence, with its significance and its bound.

**What changes from chapter 1** (Peyton Jones, *How to write a great research
paper*; Widom; Trafford & Leshem 2008, pp. 133–143):

| | Chapter 1 | Chapter 7 |
|---|---|---|
| Status | A promise | Delivered, on evidence |
| Reference | Forward to the evidence | Back to where it was argued |
| Level | The intended outcome | A conceptual conclusion: what the facts mean |
| Significance | Asserted | Argued: who changes what |
| Bound | Scope | Carried in the claim's own paragraph |

**What a contribution is.**

- **Knowledge, not activity.** The aim is "not to describe what you have done,
  but to inform readers of the successful outcome … and to convince readers of
  the validity of those conclusions" (Ernst, *How to write a technical paper*).
- **Refutable.** "We describe the WizWoz system. It is really cool" fails; "We
  prove that the type system is sound (Section 4)" passes (Peyton Jones). Test:
  could an examiner show the sentence false from the evidence?
- **An artefact counts through what it lets someone know or do.** CS conclusions
  present a product in 66.67 % of theses (Soler-Monreal Table 9), but a list of a
  system's properties "is hardly research" (EGZ p. 123).
- **A bounded or negative finding is a first-class contribution.** Crucial tests
  show "whether your hypothesis has stood up or … been demolished. Either way you
  have made progress" (EGZ p. 70). Limitations are written "not … in an
  apologetic tone" (EGZ pp. 117–118).
- **Modest at honours scale.** "A modest contribution that is plausible and can
  be defended" (Trafford & Leshem p. 141); originality "does not have to be ground
  breaking" (Golding p. 571).

**The parts of one contribution, in order** (assembled from Trafford & Leshem
pp. 141–143, Soler-Monreal's M2 steps and Peyton Jones; no single source gives
the sequence):

1. **Claim.** One refutable sentence, with chapter 1's noun.
2. **Question answered.** The sub-question it settles. "Answers to research
   questions" is the commonest M2 step, 93.75 % (Soler-Monreal Table 9).
3. **Deciding evidence.** The one fact that carries the claim, with a
   back-reference. At most one number.
4. **Significance.** Who should now think or act differently, and how ("Keep in
   mind how you expect the behavior of readers to change", Ernst). Only 20.83 %
   of CS conclusions state a product's significance (Soler-Monreal Table 9); done
   well, it is what sets a conclusion apart.
5. **Bound.** The scope it holds within, in the same paragraph. The anti-pattern
   is limitations acknowledged, then conclusions written "as though the
   limitations don't exist" (ACM SIGSOFT General Standard).

**Format.** In a thesis, one paragraph per contribution, not bullets: paper guides
want bullets in the introduction (Peyton Jones), but a conclusion wants "a short
summarizing paragraph" (Widom), and thesis corpora use prose. No source sets a
count; EGZ's single aim (p. 72) and Peyton Jones's "one ping" push towards few.

| Pitfall | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Activity list** | Delete the verbs of doing ("built", "ran"). Does a claim remain? | Ernst; Dunleavy p. 206 |
| **Artefact only** | Does it say what the artefact lets someone know or do? | Peyton Jones; SIGSOFT Engineering Research |
| **Vague value** ("insights", "useful") | Is it refutable? | Peyton Jones; Tulving & Madigan via Dunleavy p. 238 |
| **Inflation** | Does each word survive the evidence? | Trafford & Leshem p. 141; Golding pp. 571–572; SIGSOFT ("overstates the novelty") |
| **Mismatch with chapter 1** | Same items, same order, same nouns? | EGZ pp. 70, 72, 122; Golding p. 570 |
| **A new claim** | Does each sentence trace to a chapter 6 paragraph? | EGZ p. 122 |
| **Bound as an afterthought** | Is the limit in the claim's own paragraph? | SIGSOFT General |
| **The answer given twice** | Does the contribution re-answer what the preamble answered? Keep the answer in the preamble and the warrant here | Contributions pass, §6 |

### (d3) Future work: the next steps

**Job.** Name the next steps the findings and their limits point to, specifically
enough that one follow-up study could take each. Done well, it "can powerfully
communicate … your understanding of the importance and limits of your work";
done poorly, it "can appear absurd" (EGZ p. 122).

- **Two kinds of item, both scored** (UWA items 5 and 6): an item that lifts a
  recognised limitation, and an item that answers an open question the results
  raised. A list of only the first kind meets item 5 and misses item 6.
- **Anchor by pointer, spend the words on the step.** Future directions are
  "often the mirror image of the stated limitations"; "replicating the same
  information under both headings is redundant" (Brutus, Aguinis & Wassmer 2013,
  pp. 64, 67). The limitation takes one clause or a `\ref`.
- **Specific: objective and method.** Of 129 future-work statements at SOUPS
  2019, 31 were broad, 76 gave an objective, 5 a method, 17 both; of 978 citing
  papers, 7 took one up (Suray et al. 2024, §4.2). Their rule: "If Making a
  Future Work Statement, Be Specific" (§6.1).
- **One follow-up study each.** "Immediate and incremental … actionable" (Brutus
  G7, pp. 67–68). The five-year research agenda (EGZ p. 122) is a PhD-scale
  expectation.
- **Ordered by a principle.** No source prescribes an order; weight first
  ("those weaknesses that matter most", Brutus G4, p. 66) transfers. No source
  requires a lead sentence: the order may stay implicit when the items' labels
  and anchors carry it (Marc 2026-10-15). A lead earns its place only with
  content a successor needs, such as which direction comes first.
- **Format.** No source requires bullets or prose. A median of 4 statements of
  about 39 words each at SOUPS (Suray et al. §4); a designated heading makes them
  findable (§6).

**The parts of one item:**

1. **Anchor.** The limitation or open question, as a `\ref` or one clause.
2. **Objective.** What would be built, run or measured.
3. **Method.** How, with the tool, data or design named ("ways in which these
   might be … resolved", UWA).
4. **Payoff.** Which claim would change, or which decision it would settle. The
   part most often missing (Thomson 2015, "who needs to do what", via a fetch
   summary).

| Pitfall | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Wish list** | Could one follow-up study take the item? | EGZ p. 122; Suray et al. §6 |
| **Generic or trivial** ("more data", "replication", "further research is needed") | Does it name an objective *and* a method? | Suray et al. §6 |
| **Limitations restated** | Are most of the item's words about the step, not the limit? | Brutus G7 |
| **Future work as a mitigation** | Does each 6.5 threat carry its own mitigation and residual there? | Feldt & Magazinius 2010 (26.5 %, via harvest D) |
| **Unpaid promises** | Is every forward pointer in chapter 6 paid by a named item? | UWA items 5, 6; handoff 2026-10-09 §1 |
| **Reads as unfinished work** | Is each item the next step after an answer, never the missing half of one? | Thomson 2018 ("handwringing"); EGZ p. 122 allows "work … left incomplete" |
| **Unchecked "no one has"** | Is any gap claim verified against the literature? Otherwise word it as a direction | Golding p. 571 (inferred) |
| **New material** | Any argument, number or citation first met here? | Thomson 2012; EGZ p. 122 |

### (d4) The close: the last sentence

**Job.** Leave the examiner, who writes the report next, with the position the
thesis took. "Take the position" (Thomson 2018).

- **Return to the opening problem.** "A pleasing symmetry" (Thomson 2015); come
  back out to "the general, abstract or contextual proposition you established at
  the outset" (Thomson 2021); the conclusion "may well refer back to the
  Introduction" (Cambridge Pink Book §12.9).
- **Name the next step as the continuation of that problem.** "Viable directions
  in which future research might go from where your work leaves off" (Dunleavy
  p. 207). One sentence that does both meets all three corpus norms (§(f)).
- **Crisp.** Crispness "helps to give the sense of closure" (EGZ p. 123).

| Pitfall | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Ending on a deficit** | Is the last note a limitation or an apology? | Thomson 2024 ("category is limitations, part 2") |
| **Grandiose close** | Is it wider than chapter 6's claim? | Thomson 2012, 2015 |
| **Cliché or formula** ("in conclusion") | Delete the phrase. Is anything lost? | UNC Writing Center (essay genre; used only where thesis sources agree) |
| **A new claim** | Does it trace to chapter 6? | All of the above |
| **Hedge stack** | More than one hedge in the sentence? | Thomson 2018 |

## (e) Chapter-level failure modes

| Failure | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Summary, not conclusion** | Does each paragraph say what its finding means? | EGZ p. 123 |
| **Undischarged aim** | Read chapter 1's problem, its aim, then chapter 7: one logical "mini-document"? | EGZ pp. 72, 122; Mullins & Kiley p. 385 |
| **Further discussion** | Any new analysis, or literature quoted to argue? | EGZ p. 122 |
| **New material** | Any new source, number, result or idea? | Thomson 2012; UNSW; Bell 1987 via Trafford & Leshem p. 131 |
| **Contribution never claimed outright** | Is there an explicit contribution sentence? | Trafford, Leshem & Bitzer 2014 |
| **Over- or under-claiming** | Sweeping claims; or "vague or truncated" ones? | Golding p. 570; Thomson 2018 |
| **Repetitive cycles** | The same claim in more than one section? | Soler-Monreal p. 127 |
| **Soap box** | A call to action that does not flow from the evidence? | DoctoralWriting SIG |
| **Running out of steam** | Is the chapter scanty against the evidence before it? | DoctoralWriting SIG; Dunleavy p. 56 |

## (f) Where the sources disagree, and how this dissertation reconciles them

- **Bunton's move count (a correction).** Handoff 2026-10-09 §1 gives Bunton 2005
  as five moves ending on a close. That is his humanities and social-science
  model. His science-and-technology model has three moves usually present (work
  carried out; method, findings, claims and products; future research) and
  almost no closing restatement (1 of 25, 4 %; Bunton Tables 2–5 via the OSU
  excerpt and Soler-Monreal Tables 2–3). CS differs: 60.42 % close on a
  recapitulation (Soler-Monreal Table 7). *Reconciled (Marc 2026-10-15):* this
  dissertation follows Bunton's science-and-technology pattern and ends on future
  research, with no closing restatement: the preamble already takes the
  position. The one fact a successor needs, which direction comes first, leads
  the future-work list instead. (Superseded: a last sentence returning to
  chapter 1's problem.)
- **Recapping the chapters.** It is the descriptive CS norm (72.92 %), and every
  prescriptive source rejects it. *Reconciled:* a recap appears only as a
  subordinate clause carrying a claim. With one discussion chapter, there is
  nothing to cycle through.
- **Selling against restraint.** Trafford & Leshem call the chapter "a selling
  opportunity" (p. 135), and the CS community expects the contribution
  emphasised; EGZ wants it "crisp and concise". *Reconciled:* sell the warrant,
  not the size.
- **Numbers.** Widom wants claims made concrete with numbers; EGZ wants minimal
  discussion. *Reconciled:* one number chapter 6 already gave, where the claim
  turns on it.
- **Citations.** EGZ: still quoting literature means the material belongs in the
  discussion. Bunton and Thomson let the conclusion relate the findings to the
  literature. *Reconciled:* name a work already met in chapter 3 or 6 to locate
  the contribution; never a new source.
- **Future work: restate the limit or add to it?** UWA ties future work to
  shortcomings; Brutus and Suray call the restatement redundant. *Reconciled:*
  point to the limit, and spend the words on objective, method and payoff.
- **Future work: the next study or an agenda?** Brutus and Suray: one study. EGZ,
  Dunleavy and Thomson: a longer reach. *Reconciled at honours scale:* the next
  study.
- **Where future work sits.** UWA scores it under *Discussion*; Bunton and
  Soler-Monreal place it in the conclusion. *Reconciled:* it lives in 7.2, and
  6.5 points to it (harvest D, B11).
- **Reflection.** Cambridge asks Part II students what they would do
  differently; UWA does not ask, and `voice.md` would cut it. *Reconciled:* none.
- **A discrepancy inside Soler-Monreal.** The prose (p. 123) gives Move 4's steps
  as 93.75 % and 64.58 %; Table 11 gives 91.67 % and 66.67 %. Cite the table.

## (g) The checklist, run per chapter pass

1. The preamble restates the research question once, in chapter 1's words, and
   answers it in the same paragraph.
2. The answer says which possible outcome happened, and no sentence repeats the
   abstract or chapter 1.
3. Chapter 1's problem, its aim, then chapter 7 read as one logical document.
4. Every claim traces to a chapter 6 paragraph: no new number, source, result,
   float or analysis.
5. No paragraph walks the chapters; a restated result is followed by what it
   means.
6. The contributions match chapter 1's in number, order and head noun.
7. Each contribution is a refutable claim, names its sub-question, cites its
   deciding evidence (at most one number), names who changes what, and carries
   its bound in its own paragraph.
8. No question is answered twice: the answer sits in the preamble, the warrant in
   the contributions.
9. Every future-work item has an anchor, an objective, a method and a payoff, and
   one follow-up study could take it.
10. At least one item answers an open question the results raised (UWA item 6),
    not only a limitation (item 5).
11. Every forward pointer in chapter 6 is paid; no item stands in for a 6.5
    mitigation; no unverified gap claim.
12. The items follow a principle; any lead sentence carries content, not a
    description of the list.
13. The chapter's last sentence ends on a payoff, never a deficit; no cliché,
    hedge stack or claim wider than chapter 6's. A closing return to chapter 1
    is optional (Bunton vs Soler-Monreal, §(f)); this dissertation has none.
14. One tense scheme: present perfect for the work, past for what chapter 1
    asked, present for the claims (Soler-Monreal pp. 119–122; no source
    prescribes one).
15. The chapter is about 4–6 % of the word band (650–900 words), under two to four
    generic headings with an unheaded preamble.
