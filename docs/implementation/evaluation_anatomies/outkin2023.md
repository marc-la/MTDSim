# outkin2023 — evaluation-section anatomy

Source read: `docs/sources/lit_review/original/4_outkin2023defender.pdf` (18 pp., IEEE TDSC 20(3), May/June 2023, pp. 1909–1926; text extracted per page with pypdf because the PDF renderer is unavailable in this environment, so figure *images* were not viewable — chart-type statements below come from captions and the prose that describes each figure, and are marked as such) cross-checked against `docs/sources/lit_review/4_outkin2023defender.md`. Locators are PDF page numbers ("p.N", 1–18 of the file, not the journal pagination); md line numbers ("md L") where used.

Paper's own research question / purpose (one sentence): to give a defender a quantitative method for allocating detection resources across the steps of a multi-step attack of indefinite duration, by approximating GPLADD attacker–defender games as discrete-time Markov chains and reading off attack-difficulty (time-to-success) and attacker-persistence ("Ready"-residence time) metrics, demonstrated on a notional APT3 chain parameterised first from SME/literature estimates and then from MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations vendor data — "The primary practical goal of this paper is to help people use their resources as efficiently as possible (buying the right software, hiring the right people, etc.)" (p.1–2).

## A. Skeleton of the evaluation portion

Verbatim headings with page:

- "3 APPROACH" (p.5) — **setup, formal**: notation list; "3.1 GPLADD Games" (p.5); "3.2 PLADD - Building Blocks for GPLADD" (p.6); "3.3 Transitions on Attack Graph" (p.6); "3.4 Discrete-Time Markov Chain Approximation" (p.7); "3.5 Markov Chain Transition Probabilities Inference" (p.8).
- "4 SIMPLIFIED APT3 ATTACK DEFINITION" (p.9) — **setup, scenario**: "4.1 Attack Definition" (p.9); "4.2 Attacker Strategy" (p.9); "4.3 Defender Strategy" (p.9).
- "5 RESULTS" (p.10) — **results, with setup interleaved**: "5.1 Attack Metrics Sensitivity to Detection" (p.10); "5.1.1 Transition Matrix From Literature and SME Data" (p.10) — this is parameter *derivation*, i.e. setup inside Results; "5.1.2 Sensitivity Analysis" (p.11); "5.2 MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations Analysis" (p.12); "5.2.1 Detection Probabilities" (p.12) — again parameter derivation inside Results; "5.2.2 Defender Performance" (p.13).
- "6 CONCLUSION" (p.15).

Setup and results are **nominally two blocks (§3–4 vs §5)** but the numerical parameterisation of each experiment is placed inside §5 immediately before the results it feeds (§5.1.1 → §5.1.2; §5.2.1 → §5.2.2). There is no "Discussion" or "Limitations" heading; interpretation and caveats are inline in §5 and §6. "Sensitivity Analysis" is an explicit sub-heading (§5.1.2).

## B. Setup — what is declared before any result

- **Network configuration(s):** one, described qualitatively, no host count or topology: "The defender network consists of an enterprise network, an ICS network, and a DMZ (demilitarized zone subnetwork). The ICS controls an electric power system via RTUs." (p.9). Nodes are not enumerated; the model's state is the attack step, not the network. The attack graph is the structural object: a single **linear** chain of 9 success conditions c1–c9 (Eq. 10, p.9), each named with a short label: Start, Email, Link, Exec, IPEW, Msg, MvEW, RTU, Ready (p.9), drawn in Fig. 2 "APT3 attack steps" (p.9). Linear-chain restriction stated as scope: "We develop the Markov analysis and approximation for 'linear' attack graphs … The Markov representation can be extended to attacks with 'parallel' chains and conditions but is out of scope for this paper." (p.7). How chosen: "a simplified version of the APT3 techniques as described in the MITRE ATT&CK Framework" (p.9); footnote 4: "We will call this a 'notional attack' in what follows to indicate that we represent a simplified version of an APT3 attack." (p.4).
- **Attacker model / scenarios:** one attacker, one defender ("This paper treats a single attacker and a single defender." p.3). Attacker strategy declared in §4.2: an **"eager" attacker**, "defined in [4] as the attacker starting at the next attack step as soon as all the preconditions are satisfied" (p.9); attacks are organised into a **campaign** ("to represent attackers who may attempt multiple attempts at getting into a particular system", p.9); "We assume that the attacker sends just one email at any given time to avoid detection." (p.9). Attacker capability is otherwise *implicit in the transition probabilities* ("The matrix tr reflects both the attacker and defender capabilities for a given system", p.8). Two **attack variants** (Att. Chain 1, Att. Chain 2), each a mapping of the 9 GPLADD steps to ATT&CK Evaluations test-plan step ids (Table 3, p.12). Named once by table; not bold-defined. Key modelling assumptions are listed as bullets in §1 (p.3–4): time-to-success at each step "can be described by a distribution"; "Attack follows a specific attack plan … represented as attack graph".
- **Defence conditions:** the defender runs an IDS (p.9); detection at any step is **full disruption back to Start**: "we assume that detection of the attack at any stage terminates and completely disrupts that attack, and as the result the attacker moves back to the 'Start' step" (p.9; footnote 5 p.5 explains partial disruption is excluded "Because of data limitations and for simplicity"). Three **defender capability levels** defined by which ATT&CK Evaluations detection categories they can act on — Table 2 "Detection and Defender Capabilities" (p.12): Blue 0 = IOC only ("None" ambiguity); Blue 1 = IOC + Specific Alerts ("Medium"); Blue 2 = IOC + Specific + General Alerts ("Most"). Combined with the two attack variants, this yields six **"Evaluations" B10, B11, B12, B20, B21, B22** — "First number after B is the attack variant (1, 2) and second number is the defender type (0, 1, 2)." (Table 4 note, p.13). **No-defence baseline: not declared as such**, but the paper *reads* B0 as one: "B0 effectively has no defense against the attack" (p.13). In the SME-parameterised experiment (§5.1) there is a single defender whose per-step detection is then improved stage by stage (Fig. 6). No MTD mechanism; the "defence" is detection probability per step (the paper mentions segmentation as a possible mitigation, p.9, but does not model it).
- **Parameter table: yes, several, all of them transition/probability matrices rather than a columnar "parameter | value | source" table.** Table 1 (p.10): 9×9 Markov transition matrix from SME/literature, dt = 1 hr, footnoted "The transition probabilities were inferred from the available literature and/or based on subject matter expert opinions. The time step for which the probabilities were evaluated is 1 hour." Table 4 (p.13): detection probability by attack step (9 columns) for each of the six Evaluations. Table 5 (p.13): the 9×9 transition matrix for B10. The only literature-sourced number named explicitly is "about 30% of phishing emails are opened by their targets" [54] (p.10). **Distributions declared:** in the general method, time-to-success per step is a distribution f(t) (continuous or discrete, or a delta; p.3, p.6); the transition probability per step is obtained "by integrating the corresponding time-to-success distribution f(t) for each step starting at zero and with the corresponding time step duration as the upper bound", multiplied by no-detection probability under an independence assumption (p.8). For the MITRE-data experiment, distributions are skipped altogether: "we estimate the Markov chain parameters directly from the MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations data and skip integrating the time-to-success distributions" (p.12); consequently "the stay-in-place probability is not present in the MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations data and is not treated in this section. This can be interpreted as the attack time step being so large that either attack success or detection will certainly happen." (p.12). Per-step detection probability derivation is fully declared: sub-step probability = "the number of detections … by unique vendors divided by the total number of vendors (twelve)"; step probability = "the ceiling of the probabilities of its sub-steps"; defender-level probability = "the ceiling of the probabilities of detection for their relevant detection categories" (p.12).
- **Replications:** essentially **none, by design** — the quantities are computed analytically from the chain (steady-state distribution; first-passage-time distribution), not by repeated simulation: "we use the steady state distribution of the Markov chain to represent the fraction of the time an attacker spends at a particular step" (p.2); "Given the corresponding Markov chain transition probabilities, we can calculate the steady state of the Markov chain and a measure of the time-to-success distribution." (p.13). One simulated trajectory is shown as illustration: Fig. 4 "illustrates a single realization of the attack, that may differ between different realizations" (p.11). Number of simulated realizations, seeds: **not stated**. Horizon: the game is "of indefinite duration" (p.1, p.3); the Fig. 4 realization runs past time step 400 (p.11). Termination rule: none (indefinite); "success" is first passage to "Ready" (p.13). Time step dt = 1 hr for the SME matrix (p.10); "This duration does not need to be the same for different attack steps. It is assumed to be the same for all attack steps for simplicity." (footnote 10, p.8).
- **Statistics:** the outputs are whole distributions (first-passage-time distributions, Figs. 5, 13–16) and steady-state vectors (Figs. 10–12), plus point probabilities read off them in prose ("about 4%", "about 80%", "about 10%", "about 6%", "about 20%", "about 2% … about 5%", pp.11–15). No mean/std/CI/min–max/significance tests. Uncertainty is acknowledged verbally, not quantified: "Certain parameters can also be informed in the future by cyber and emulation-based experimentation and refined with uncertainty quantification." (p.11).
- **Anything else declared:** tools — "Mathematica for graph representation, Markov chain analysis and simulation; Python for MITRE data analysis and detection probability estimation." (p.10). Data source: MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations APT3 round, 12 vendors (p.12; footnote 3 GitHub URL p.2). No hardware, run times, or code release stated.

## C. Results — how the numbers are shown

- **Order of results:** (1) §5.1 SME-parameterised notional attack: first the transition matrix (Table 1), then one sample trajectory (Fig. 4), then the first-passage-time distribution (Fig. 5), then the sensitivity of Ready-residence to per-stage detection improvement (Fig. 6) (pp.10–11). (2) §5.2 MITRE-parameterised: detection-probability tables (Tables 2–5), then per-defender transition diagrams (Figs. 7–9), then steady-state distributions (Figs. 10–12), then time-to-success distributions (Figs. 13–16) (pp.12–15). Within §5.2, ordering is by defender capability ascending (B0 → B1 → B2) for attack variant 2, with variant 1 dismissed as "Qualitatively … very similar" (p.13), except one variant-1 figure (Fig. 16, B12) introduced last to make the resource-allocation point. The order is method-first (show the object: matrix → diagram → steady state → distribution), then comparison.
- **Every figure in the evaluation portion** (captions verbatim; chart type inferred from prose, images not viewable here):
  - Fig. 2 "APT3 attack steps." (p.9) — attack-graph diagram of c1…c9 (setup).
  - Fig. 3 "A Markov chain representation of notional attack." (p.10) — transition diagram (nodes = steps, edges = probabilities), the graphical twin of Table 1 ("an alternative and equivalent way to represent the same information", p.10).
  - Fig. 4 "A simulation of attack state over time." (p.11) — single realization; x = time step (at least to >400), y = attack step 1–9 (per prose, p.11); one series.
  - Fig. 5 "The distribution of the time of the first passage to the 'Ready' state." (p.11) — distribution plot; x = "effectively the number of steps in all the attacks done as a part of the attack campaign until success" (p.11); y = probability; one series.
  - Fig. 6 "Declines in the attack time spent in the 'Ready' state versus investments corresponding to detection at specific stages of an attack." (p.11) — the sensitivity figure; series = one per attack stage whose detection is improved (per caption/prose); x = detection investment/improvement (range and increments **not stated** in text); y = Ready-residence fraction.
  - Fig. 7 "The transition diagram for Defender B0 and Evaluation B20 from Table 2." (p.13); Fig. 8 "The transition diagram for evaluation B21 for defender B1." (p.13); Fig. 9 "The transition diagram for evaluation B22 for defender B2." (p.13) — three transition-diagram graphs, one per defender level, compared by *structure* ("has more ways that B1 can disrupt the attack than B20", p.13).
  - Fig. 10 "The Markov chain steady state distribution for evaluation B20 and defender B0." (p.14); Fig. 11 "… for the evaluation B21 and defender B1." (p.14); Fig. 12 "… for evaluation B22 and defender B2." (p.14) — steady-state distributions over the 9 states, one per defender; read as bar-like distributions (all weight on Ready for B0; "about 5%" on Ready for B1; "about 2%" for B2, p.14).
  - Fig. 13 "The time to success for evaluation B20 and defender B0." (p.14); Fig. 14 "… for the evaluation B21 and defender B1." (p.14); Fig. 15 "… for the evaluation B22 and defender B2." (p.15); Fig. 16 "The time to success for the evaluation B12 and defender B2." (p.15) — first-passage-time distributions, one per Evaluation; compared by the mass at the minimum length ("succeeds completely unimpeded", p.15) and by shape ("non-monotonic … giving an appearance of a mixture of distributions", p.15).
  One figure per (condition × metric); no multi-series overlay of conditions anywhere in the results; comparison is done by the reader across adjacent single-condition figures and by the prose.
- **Every table:**
  - Table 1 "The Markov Process Transition Matrix Showing the Transition Probability for Each Attack Step" (p.10) — 9×9, SME/literature-derived, dt = 1 hr; the §5.1 input.
  - Table 2 "Detection and Defender Capabilities" (p.12) — 3 rows (Blue 0/1/2) × 3 columns (Defender Name | Level of Ambiguity | Detection Categories); defines the defence conditions.
  - Table 3 "Attack Chains Used in the Analysis" (p.12) — 9 rows × 4 columns (Step | GPLADD Index | Att. Chain 1 | Att. Chain 2), mapping model steps to ATT&CK Evaluations test-plan ids (steps 1–3 have no mapping). Note: the last row is printed as step "8 Ready" (a typographical duplication of 8; p.12).
  - Table 4 "Detection Probability by Attack Step for Each Evaluation (E)" (p.13) — 6 rows (B10…B22) × 9 step columns; the §5.2 input; the paper's closest thing to a condition-by-parameter table.
  - Table 5 "The Markov Process Transition Matrix for Defender B0 and Evaluation B10" (p.13) — 9×9, showing the near-degenerate chain (only tr6,1 = 0.08).
- **How the baseline appears:** B0 (B10/B20) is presented first in each figure triple (Figs. 7, 10, 13) and described as the no-defence case in prose: "B0 effectively has no defense against the attack - the steady state distribution as shown in Fig. 10 has all its weight on the 'Ready' state." (p.13); "defender B0 is largely unable to stop the attacker progress: about 80% of the time the attacker succeeds completely unimpeded." (p.15). It is never overlaid with B1/B2 in one chart.
- **How comparisons are stated in prose:** as approximate probabilities and fractions with "about": "about 2% of the time allocated to the 'Ready' state as compared to about 5% of time … in Fig. 11" (p.14); "the attacker succeeds unimpeded with a probability of only about 10% and in the evaluation B22, this probability further drops to about 6%" (p.15); "about 20% in evaluation B12 and with probability of only about 6% in evaluation B22" (p.15). Ordering statements: "Defender B2 (Fig. 9) has the best capability of all three." (p.13). Materiality is explicitly deferred to the defender: "Whether the difference between 5% versus 2% in 'Ready' state is material would be determined by the defender preferences, cost difference between B1 and B2 capabilities, and the nature of the system the defender is protecting." (p.14). Recommendation-form: "from the cost-benefit point of view, it is best to detect the attack at the last, 'Ready', state … However, this may not be optimal from the risk-minimization point of view" (p.11); "optimized effort allocation by defenders … across different parts of their system may be more important than making a single step substantially more difficult" (p.15). No percentage-reduction phrasing ("reduced by X%").

## D. Narrative — the claims, in order

§5.1 (SME parameterisation):
1. Parameters are "rough approximations for three reasons" — averages of current practice; vary by defender capability; change with capability improvements (p.10–11) — stated *before* any result.
2. A single realization shows the attacker "spends a lot of time at the steps 1 - 3 … and only occasionally reaches the steps 4, 5, 6, and 8, only to be quickly dislodged … When the attacker finally reaches the step 9 'Ready', he stays there for a while, because of the low detection probability in that state." (p.11).
3. "Given enough time … the attacker eventually succeeds with high probability in this example." but "the probability of the attack succeeding undetected … is small, perhaps about 4%." (p.11).
4. From Fig. 6: "it is best to detect the attack at the last, 'Ready', state" on cost-benefit grounds, with the risk-minimisation counter-caveat (p.11).
5. "making a single step in the attack difficult generally does not have a strong effect on the fraction of the time … in the 'Ready' state, because once that difficult step is passed, there is nothing else that stops the attack"; conjecture that adding more failure/push-back points "may be an effective heuristic for defender optimization." (p.11).

§5.2 (MITRE parameterisation) — the three headline inferences are stated up front (p.13): "1) … even state of the art capabilities available on the market at the time of the original Evaluations, may not be able to prevent certain APT threats from succeeding; 2) there is significant variation in outcomes between defenders of different capabilities and outcomes; 3) the ability to increase the difficulty of the attack at a single step is insufficient to make the overall attack difficult." Then, in order:
6. Transition-diagram structure differs by defender: B0 "can detect and disrupt the attack only at the earlier attack stages; once the attacker succeeds at the step 6, there is no chance" (p.13); B1 "has more ways … to disrupt"; B2 "able to roll back the attacker progress at most steps" (p.13).
7. Steady states "paint even more drastic difference": B0 all weight on Ready (p.13); B2 "it is the attacker who appears to be at a significant disadvantage" (p.14); B2 ≈ 2% vs B1 ≈ 5% on Ready, attributed to B1's inability to detect at stages 7–8 and B2's weak detection there (8%, 42%) (p.14).
8. Operational-relevance check via time-to-success (first passage) because "the convergence time to steady state may be long" (p.14): B0 ≈ 80% unimpeded; B1 ≈ 10%; B2 ≈ 6% (p.15).
9. Cross-variant comparison B12 vs B22 for the same defender B2: higher detection at Ready (67% vs 42%) "alone is insufficient to degrade the attacker success" — ≈ 20% vs ≈ 6% quick success — hence "optimized effort allocation … may be more important than making a single step substantially more difficult" (p.15).
10. Observation on shape: B1 and B2 "exhibit non-monotonic time-to-success distributions … giving an appearance of a mixture of distributions", explanation conjectured and deferred (p.15).

**Claims about the ATTACKER's properties from the data:** yes, pervasively — the paper's two headline metrics are attacker-side quantities: attack difficulty (time-to-success) and "the attacker ability to remain hidden in the system after attack succeeds" (Ready-residence time) (p.2). Data-derived attacker statements include: the ≈4% undetected-success probability (p.11); "the attacker who appears to be at a significant disadvantage" under B2 (p.14); "the attacker succeeds very quickly with probability of about 20% in evaluation B12" (p.15); the attacker "spending the most of their time trying to regain a foothold" under capable defenders (p.16). The paper is explicit that attacker capability is fixed by the data used: "This ATT&CK Evaluations data was empirically generated for a given attacker capability. If the data for different attacker capabilities is available, it can be incorporated into this analysis seamlessly" (p.12).

## E. Sensitivity / parameter analysis

**Present, in two forms, and one of them is a titled sub-section.**

1. **§5.1.2 "Sensitivity Analysis" (p.11), Fig. 6.** Parameter varied: the detection probability at a specific attack stage ("investments corresponding to detection at specific stages of an attack", Fig. 6 caption). Design: **one-at-a-time by stage** — each series improves detection at one stage while others stay at the Table 1 values (this is the reading forced by the caption and by "improved detection at specific stages of an attack", p.11; the text does not say it in those words). Range and step size of the improvement: **not stated**. Which stages: **not stated** in text (the figure legend was not extractable). Range justification: **not stated**. Reported as: one figure (Fig. 6), placed inside Results under its own sub-heading; no table. Claim it supports: (a) "it is best to detect the attack at the last, 'Ready', state" on cost-benefit grounds, with the explicit risk counter-argument (p.11); (b) "making a single step in the attack difficult generally does not have a strong effect on the fraction of the time … 'Ready'" (p.11). The paper frames this as the mechanism of its contribution — "We specifically quantify the effects of a defender allocating more resources for detection at specific attack steps by calculating the sensitivity of the Ready-residence time to such changes." (p.2) — and lists "An approach to estimate attack metric sensitivity to different defender resource allocation" among its contributions (p.3).

2. **§5.2 as a 3 × 2 factorial** (defender level Blue 0/1/2 × attack variant 1/2 = six Evaluations, Table 4 p.13). This is a **grid**, not OAT, but the levels are *empirically given* (derived from ATT&CK Evaluations categories), not swept over a range; the paper does not call it a sensitivity analysis. Reported as: three figure triples (transition diagram / steady state / time-to-success per defender) for variant 2, plus one variant-1 figure (Fig. 16). Range justification: the three levels are justified by "the level of ambiguity that human defenders would be required to deal with" mapped from the Evaluations' detection categories (p.12). Claim supported: inferences 1–3 on p.13 and the B12-vs-B22 allocation argument (p.15).

**Sensitivity that is named but not performed:** the time step dt ("The transition probabilities are dependent on the time step duration, because longer time-step duration weekly monotonically increases the probabilities of both success and detection", p.8) — fixed at 1 hr, not varied; the ceiling aggregation rule (p.12) — not varied; partial vs full disruption (footnote 5, p.5; p.9) — full only; the independence approximation for success × no-detection ("we believe this level of precision is not needed in this paper", p.8). The Conclusion asserts the method "can also allow identifying the parameters whose uncertainty has the greatest effect on attack success metrics" (p.16) but no such ranking is reported.

**Where it sits:** §5.1.2 is its own titled sub-section inside Results; the factorial comparison is §5.2.2 "Defender Performance"; nothing in an appendix.

## F. Discussion / limitations

- **No separate Discussion or Limitations section.** Interpretation sits inline in §5.1.2 and §5.2.2; the Conclusion (§6, p.15–16) restates findings and lists extensions. Threats to validity are expressed as inline caveats at the point of use. Verbatim limitation sentences worth keeping:
  - On parameter provenance (p.10–11): "At this point, they should be treated as rough approximations for three reasons: 1) they are based on the available data and SME opinion about such probabilities in general and as such can only reflect the averages describing the current and recent state of practice in cyber security; 2) they vary significantly between different defender capabilities as shown in Section 5.2.1; 3) they can change with improvements in defensive or offensive capabilities."
  - On the meaning of results under uncalibrated data (p.8): "If the data combined from different sources is used consistently and carefully, the results preserve the attack metrics ranking and can be used to improve the defender posture even if the results are not fully calibrated or if there is missing data for parts of the attack." and (p.8) "Even if some data is missing or imprecise, attacks and defender strategies can still be compared and ranked for the same defender and the same system."
  - On timing realism (p.14): "The times to success shown here are somewhat notional. A key component of the time is to allow artifacts generated by the adversary behaviors to register in the necessary logs … The time required for information to percolate from logging to analysis can also vary widely between defenders, but we did not have data to incorporate this variability into our analysis. Despite this limitation of the analysis, we believe these distributions are still informative for defender decision making, because of substantial differences in the shape of the distributions."
  - On the approximation (p.8): "This calculation is approximate because we are multiplying the success probability by the no-detection probability for the entire time interval … Given that the probabilities we use are estimated, we believe this level of precision is not needed in this paper."
  - On full-disruption assumption (footnote 5, p.5): "In practice the defender may not have the capability to disrupt the attack completely … Because of data limitations and for simplicity, we only consider full attack disruption here."; and (p.9) "Relaxing this assumption and allowing the attacker to persist at previous steps of the attack will not change the analysis methodology but requires a more extensive data gathering effort."
  - On the missing stay-in-place probability in the MITRE experiment (p.12): "This can be interpreted as the attack time step being so large that either attack success or detection will certainly happen."
  - On scope (p.3): "This paper treats a single attacker and a single defender."; (p.2) "Analysis of correlated detection is out of scope of this paper."; (p.7) parallel chains out of scope.
  - On an unexplained result (p.15): "We believe this is because the probability of all possible pathways to attack success that have the same length does not need to be a monotonically decreasing function of the pathway length but leave the exploration of this phenomenon to the future work."
- **Attacker-realism concessions:** the attack is "notional"/"simplified" (footnote 4 p.4; p.8–9); attacker capability is whatever the ATT&CK Evaluations emulation embodied (p.12); the "eager" strategy is asserted not evidenced (p.9); the linear chain "does not uniquely determine the attack pathways" (p.9).
- **Comparability caveats:** the paper compares defenders only against "the same attack and the same attacker" (p.9) and warns that materiality of a 5% vs 2% difference is a defender-preference question (p.14). Two data sources "can be combined in a single analysis, but this is outside of the scope of this paper." (p.8).

## G. Transferable vs purpose-specific

**Transfers to an honours dissertation evaluating existing MTD mechanisms against a new attacker model on a simulator:**
- A titled "Sensitivity Analysis" sub-section inside Results, placed immediately after the parameter-derivation sub-section it depends on (§5.1.1 → §5.1.2), so provenance and sweep are adjacent (p.10–11).
- Declaring parameter provenance *and* its status in the same breath ("rough approximations for three reasons", p.10–11) before the first result, and separately stating what a result is licensed to mean under that provenance ("preserve the attack metrics ranking", p.8) — i.e. the ranking-not-calibration framing.
- Stating the headline inferences at the top of the comparative sub-section (three numbered inferences, p.13) and then walking the figures in a fixed order per condition (structure → steady state → distribution).
- One consistent condition-naming scheme (B{variant}{defender}) defined in a table note and used for every figure and sentence (Table 4, p.13).
- Reading the weakest defender as the reference and saying so in prose ("effectively has no defense", p.13) even without a formal no-defence condition.
- Checking whether a long-run metric is operationally relevant by pairing it with a transient one ("the convergence time to steady state may be long", p.14) — the steady-state / first-passage pairing generalises to any long-run vs time-to-event pair.
- Deferring materiality to the reader's preferences explicitly (p.14) rather than asserting significance.
- Reporting whole distributions (time-to-success) rather than only means, and commenting on shape (non-monotonicity, p.15).
- Attributing a difference to the declared parameter that could explain it ("This difference is because the defender B1 cannot detect the attacker at stages 7 and 8", p.14).

**Artefacts of this paper's own purpose/venue:**
- Analytic Markov-chain outputs (steady state, first passage) in place of replicated simulation runs — no seeds, no CIs, one illustrative trajectory (Fig. 4). Not transferable to a stochastic simulator evaluation.
- The Ready-residence-time metric and the "indefinite duration" framing (p.1–3) — tied to the resource-allocation objective.
- Detection-probability derivation from ATT&CK Evaluations vendor counts with a "ceiling" aggregation (p.12) — data-specific.
- Defender "levels" defined by alert-ambiguity categories (Table 2) — a TDSC/enterprise-SOC framing, not an MTD-mechanism comparison.
- No MTD mechanism at all; "defence" is a detection-probability vector; no scheduler/strategy parameters.
- One figure per condition (Figs. 7–16) rather than overlaid series — a consequence of the outputs being graphs and distributions over 9 states.
- The unstated Fig. 6 range and stage list — a gap, not a convention to reproduce.
