# masud2025 — evaluation-section anatomy

Source read: docs/sources/lit_review/original/1.3_masud2025vulnerability.pdf (PDF pp. 4–7 for §3 setup; pp. 9–11 for §3.6 MTD definitions; pp. 12–16 for §4–§6; 17 pages total, journal pagination = PDF pagination) and docs/sources/lit_review/1_3_masud2025vulnerability.md (md lines 152–330 setup, 560–693 results/discussion/conclusion). Locators are page numbers (PDF) or line numbers (md) — every claim below carries one. Caution on the md: heading order is scrambled in places (md lists `3.2` at 166 before `3.1` at 186, and `4.6` at 620 before `4.5` at 622); PDF order is authoritative and is what §A records. Figure captions are not reliably present in the md, so figures are located by PDF page only.
Paper's own research question / purpose (one sentence, so its conventions are read against its purpose, not ours): to propose a hybrid (shuffle + diversity + redundancy) "Vulnerability Defence method" for IoT built on a three-layer Temporal HARM with a time-interval trigger mechanism, and to show through a simulated evaluation that "combining MTD techniques from different categories, such as shuffle and diversity, generally produces more favorable outcomes, including a lower probability of attack success, lower attack risk and higher attack cost" (abstract, md 22–33; PDF p. 1). Stated objectives include "Experimental validation conducted in a simulated IoT environment, demonstrating significant reductions in ASP, increases in AC, and overall improvements in network resilience" and "A defense-adversary interaction model that evaluates how MTD strategies dynamically disrupt attacker workflows" (PDF p. 2, txt 275–282).

## B. Setup — what is declared before any result

- **Network configuration(s):** one small fixed configuration plus one scaled configuration. Small: "The framework proposed in Fig. 2 contains n hosts, where n = 8. The eight hosts were selected to satisfy the system's design requirements to achieve optimal performance and resource allocation. This arrangement was intended to create a manageable yet complex environment to test the effectiveness of the MTD techniques" (PDF p. 4; md 154). Each VM "manages two services"; V8 "exclusively connects to the database (DB), making it the prime target" (PDF p. 5; md 172). Bulleted assumptions (PDF p. 5): all hosts continuously operational; max four VMs per host; V1–V4, V6 migrate across Host1–3; V5 across Host1, 2, 4; V7 across Host1–4; V8 "ineligible for migration". Topology drawn in Fig. 1 "Installing and configuring the virtualized host network" (PDF p. 5) and Fig. 3 (PDF p. 8). Large: "The network comprises 400 VMs with a cloud-bound node ... only a select few VMs serve as entry points ... All VMs in the cloud-band nodes mostly utilize Windows as the main OS and Linux as the backup OS" (PDF p. 14; md 624); sizes 50/100/200/400 VMs appear on the Fig. 8 x-axis (PDF p. 15). For IPV/PSV a third family of sizes n = 10, 20, 50, 100, 200 hosts appears in Fig. 5 (PDF p. 12) with no setup statement for how those networks were generated. How chosen: only the "design requirements" sentence above; **not otherwise stated**.
- **Attacker model / scenarios:** one attacker, defined in `3.1. Threat model` (PDF p. 5; md 188): "we assume that a potential attacker is external to the private cloud and could exploit vulnerabilities present in IoT device operating systems to gain unauthorized access. The hypervisors are assumed to provide sufficient isolation ... The attacker's objective is to breach the database (DB) on host8". Vulnerability classes scoped to "bypass and remote access vulnerabilities" (md 160) with real CVEs enumerated in Table 1 (OS: Windows 11 w1–w6, Linux li1–li6, FreeBSD f1–f6) and Table 2 (services SN1–SN14), each with CVSS base score, impact, exploitability and an "Attack cost" column (PDF p. 6). In §3.3 the attacker is additionally described in narrative form, with no parameters: "Attackers were modeled after techniques in the cyber kill chain and MITRE ATT&CK, mirroring real threats (Hutchins et al., 2011). ... Tests included widespread and focused attacks aiming to breach many or specific high-value hosts. Vulnerabilities were prioritized by return on attack, emphasizing those with greatest impact. Successful exploitation let adversaries control internals through command channels to connected devices" (PDF p. 7; md 327). No scenario names, no bold definition, no count of scenarios; "widespread" vs "focused" attacks are mentioned once and never appear in the results.
- **Defence conditions:** three mechanisms — Shuffle implemented as VM live migration (VM-LM) (§3.6.2, PDF p. 10), Diversity as OS swap ("we use Linux as the backup OS of Windows 11, Windows 11 backup OS FreeBSD, and FreeBSD backup Linux", PDF p. 13; md 590), Redundancy as "creating r replicas on a VM" (PDF p. 13; md 602) — plus combinations S+D and S+D+R. Scheduling: "Periodic triggers initiate MTD processes, exclusively designed to be proactive without reactive considerations" with a priority queue and a mutual-exclusion rule ("redundancy and shuffling both alter network paths and thus cannot run simultaneously ... diversity operates independently within the service layer", PDF p. 5; md 192). Target-selection rule: the VM to move/diversify is chosen by rank from Algorithm 3 / Eq. 8 ("VM V5 obtained the highest rank", PDF p. 13; md 582). **No-defence baseline:** partially — "Before Applying MTD" is a series in Fig. 8 (PDF p. 15) and "Before Diversity" in Fig. 7 (PDF p. 14); Fig. 6 and Fig. 9 have no no-MTD series. Table 4 lists initial (pre-MTD) per-VM ASP/AC/AR values (PDF p. 7).
- **Parameter table:** **yes** — Table 4 "System parameters and initial VM security metrics" (PDF p. 7; md 305). Two columns (Parameter, Value); rows: Number of hosts 8; Maximum VMs per host 4; Maximum service per VM 2; VM vulnerability types Remote Access, Bypass; Migration capability "Enabled for V1–V7, fixed for V8"; IP addressing scheme "Dynamic with shuffling intervals"; Defense trigger interval "10 m"; VM Metrics (V1…V8) ASP/AC/AR vectors. Also Table 3 "Symbols, explanations, and formulas" (PDF p. 7) and Tables 1–2 (CVE parameters, PDF p. 6). No probability distributions declared; all vulnerability inputs are point CVSS values from NVD/CVE (Table 1 notes, PDF p. 6). Shuffle interval "e.g., every ten minutes" (PDF p. 6); the time axes in Figs. 5 and 9 (0–140 "seconds", 0–100 unlabelled units) are not reconciled with the 10 min trigger interval.
- **Replications:** **not stated.** No runs, seeds, episodes or repetitions anywhere in §3–§4. Horizon: Fig. 5 time axis 0–140 s (PDF p. 12), Fig. 9 time axis 0–100 (PDF p. 16); termination rule not stated.
- **Statistics:** none — no mean/std/CI/tests. Results are single values per condition (worked-example path sums, e.g. "R_ap1 = R_V2 + R_V7 + R_V5 + R_V8 = 16.28 + 8.67 + 12.58 + 5.62 = 43.15", PDF p. 13; md 584) and single trajectories per VM.
- **Anything else declared:** implementation platform, language, hardware, versions — **not stated**. SDN controller / vIP mechanism described as design (Algorithm 1, PDF pp. 5–6). "Data availability: No data was used for the research described in the article" (PDF p. 16; md 691). The §3.3 "validation" paragraph asserts outcomes in past tense before any figure ("Watching strategies interact with adversaries showed how movement disrupted plans ... These fluid defenses complicated enemy progress, slowing or thwarting compromises. Data after each change evaluated reduced probability of success, greater costs, and strengthened security overall", PDF p. 7; md 327).

## C. Results — how the numbers are shown

- **Order of results:** (1) shuffle-variability metrics IPV/PSV vs network size and time (§4.1); (2) one mechanism at a time — shuffle (§4.2), diversity (§4.3), redundancy (§4.4) — each as a *worked example on V5* giving path-level and network-level numbers, then the combined Fig. 6; (3) scale-up to 50–400 VMs with all five conditions and a no-MTD bar (§4.5); (4) time-series per mechanism (Fig. 9) inside a literature-comparison subsection (§4.6). The ordering follows the order the mechanisms were introduced in §3.6, i.e. by mechanism rather than by metric or by research question. A results preamble (PDF p. 12; md 562–564) restates the IoT framing and pre-announces the finding. The headline combined-benefit claim is also given *before* §4 in the closing paragraph of §3.6.6 (PDF p. 12; md 570): "Our data show that combining shuffle and diversity boosts AC while decreasing RoA value ... combining the three basic MTD techniques ... not only reduces AR and RoA but also enhances overall network reliability."
- **Figures in the evaluation portion** (PDF pp. 12–16):
  - **Fig. 5** (PDF p. 12): three stacked line charts. (a) x = Time (seconds) 0–140, y = IPV 0–1, series n = 10, 20, 50, 100, 200 (legend headed "Column"); (b) x = VM 1–8, y = PSV 0–1, series n = 20, 15, 10 (legend headed "Edge set"); (c) x = Time 0–140 s, y = IPV+PSV 0–1, series n = 10 … 200 (legend "Levels"). Caption: "Analysis of IPV and PSV with varying host numbers (n), showing IPV with respect to time (a), PSV with respect to VM (b), and combined IPV+PSV with respect to time (c)."
  - **Fig. 6** (PDF p. 14): three line-with-marker panels (a) AR, (b) AC, (c) RoC; x = VM in the order V5, V4, V7, V2, V1, V3, V6 (seven VMs; V8 absent); five series per panel: s, d, r, s+d, s+d+r (legend e.g. "ARs, ARd, ARr, ARs+d, ARs+d+r"). No no-MTD series. Caption: "The changes in network security metrics following the implementation of shuffling strategy, diversity strategy and redundancy on each VM in the IoT cloud network."
  - **Fig. 7** (PDF p. 14): grouped bar chart titled "Attack Successful Probability (ASP)"; x = Path 1 … Path 8; y = ASP 0–0.5; two series "Before Diversity" / "After Diversity". Caption: "Impact of Diversity on Attack Success Probability (ASP)."
  - **Fig. 8** (PDF p. 15): three stacked grouped-bar panels (a) Risk Values (×1e6), (b) Cost Values (×1e6), (c) ROA Values; x = VMs 400, 200, 100, 50; six series: Before Applying MTD, After Applying S, D, R, S+D, S+D+R. Caption: "Comparing network security metrics before and after implementing four MTD strategies."
  - **Fig. 9** (PDF p. 16): 4 × 3 grid of small line charts; rows AC, AR, RoC, ASP; columns Shuffling, Diversity, Redundancy; x = Time 0–100; eight series per panel VM1–VM8. Caption: "Performance comparison of MTD shuffling, diversity, and redundancy strategies for VMs over time."
- **Tables in the evaluation portion:** **Table 5** (PDF p. 15; md 634) "Comparison of MTD papers based on integration of MTD strategies and security metrics" — 4 rows (This paper*, Alavizadeh 2020, 2022, 2021) × columns MTD techniques, Combination strategies, Security metrics (AP, AR, AC, RoC, NC), Vulnerability defense model, Considered node (VM, Service); tick/cross cells. It compares *feature coverage* against prior work, not numbers. (Referenced in §4.4 as showing "impacts of the redundancy method on individual VMs", PDF p. 13; md 604 — the table does not contain that.)
- **How the baseline appears:** as a "Before" series in Fig. 7 and Fig. 8 only; as the Table 4 initial-metric vector in setup; absent from Figs. 5, 6, 9. No prose statement of the form "compared with no MTD, X changes by Y" except via the Fig. 7/8 descriptions.
- **How comparisons are stated in prose:**
  - Magnitude, for the variability metrics: "During the 0 to 140 s window, the Ingress Protection Value (IPV) for a sample size of 20 is 0.4. This means that the IP-shuffling method renders 40% of reconnaissance attempts ineffective throughout this period" (PDF p. 12; md 572); "The PSV value for VM 3 increases from 0.5 to 1 when n = 10" (md 572).
  - Absolute worked-example numbers, per mechanism: "R_s = 167.38, AC_s = 666.2, and RoC_s = 1.01" (PDF p. 13; md 586); "R_d = 163.73, AC_d = 624.73, and RoC_d = 1.04" (md 594); "R_r = 260.84, AC_r = 878.3, and RoC_r = 1.46" (md 604) — reported but never subtracted or ratioed against each other or the baseline.
  - Ordering / qualitative: "the diversity technique appears to reduce the ASP for most paths ... Paths 3, 5, and 6 show notable reductions ... Path 7 and Path 8 exhibit little to no change" (PDF p. 13; md 598); "The R and RoA rates show higher decreases with technique D ... implying that technique R produces better results for increasing attack costs ... The combo of S+D regularly shows considerable overall performance" (PDF p. 14; md 614); "Network shuffle-based tactics frequently beat diversity-based ones ... Shuffle and Diversity turns out to be the most successful tactic" (conclusion, PDF p. 16; md 693).
  - Recommendation: "using many categories of MTD techniques is more effective than using fewer categories" (PDF p. 15; md 669); "Cloud providers can strategically choose and implement preferred MTD approaches by evaluating the resources" (PDF p. 16; md 693).

## D. Narrative — the claims, in order

1. (§3.6.6, before results) Shuffle "reduces system risk and AC value. It would, however, increase the possibility of an attacker creating a scheme to breach the system. However, diversification boosts AC without affecting system risk"; S+D "boosts AC while decreasing RoA"; adding R "introduces additional network vulnerabilities" but "improves network dependability, as evaluated by NCMs" (PDF p. 12; md 570).
2. (§4.1) IPV "exhibits minimal variability following each shuffling event"; IPV higher in smaller networks "because smaller networks have a reduced probability of having overlapping network states"; n = 20 gives IPV 0.4 ⇒ "40% of reconnaissance attempts ineffective" (PDF p. 12; md 572). PSV "rises significantly as the number of hosts decreases, reaching 1 ... for a host size of 10". IPV+PSV: "smaller networks benefit more from IP-shuffling techniques" (md 574).
3. (§4.2) After shuffling V5 from host 2 to host 4, "improvements in all security metrics"; and an *attacker-incentive* reading of RoC: "the attacker has fewer incentives to target that particular IoT cloud configuration when RoC_s is low. On the other hand, a higher RoC_s means that the attacker is more likely to attempt the same attack again, given it was previously successful" (PDF p. 13; md 586).
4. (§4.3) Diversity "does not modify or alter any attack pathways in the THARM's top layer. Instead, adding new vulnerabilities adds heterogeneity to the THARM's lower layer" (md 590); "Utilizing an alternate OS for diversity implementation can substantially influence security metrics, especially regarding the reliance on vulnerability data that the new OS introduces" (md 594); ASP reduced on most paths, unchanged on Paths 7–8; "the technique's success may vary across different attack paths" (md 598).
5. (§4.4) Redundancy on V5 raises R, AC and RoC (R_r = 260.84 etc.); "combining MTD techniques from different categories, such as shuffle and diversity, generally produces more favorable outcomes as seen in Fig. 6, including a lower probability of attack success, lower attack risk, and higher attack cost" (PDF p. 13; md 606).
6. (§4.5) At 50–400 VMs: "With increasing cloud-band sizes, we see a linear growth in all indicators. Although the measure R performs poorly for D and ROA alone, its effectiveness improves when combined with S+D+R. The combo of S+D regularly shows considerable overall performance" (PDF p. 14; md 614).
7. (§4.6) Over time, "Attack Cost usually increases exponentially over time. On the contrary ... Attack Risk, RoC, and ASP ... are dynamic properties that reflect a constantly changing security environment"; "each of the subsequent MTD changes means that attackers will have to change their strategies: in turn, it needs more money, impacts success rate and affects efficiency" (PDF pp. 14–15; md 628, 657). Prior work "do[es] not consider time ... security outcomes can be distinguishably different from those calculated for static approaches. This difference then proves the contribution of dynamic strategies" (md 659).
8. (§5) "One of the key discoveries from our experiment involves the potency of diversification" (PDF p. 15; md 665); "Comparing the combined approaches' results with individual MTD results demonstrates that using many categories of MTD techniques is more effective than using fewer categories" (md 669).
9. (§6) "Network shuffle-based tactics frequently beat diversity-based ones ... Notably, Shuffle and Diversity turns out to be the most successful tactic" (PDF p. 16; md 693).

**Claims about the attacker from the data:** yes, but interpretive rather than measured. (i) RoC is read as attacker *incentive*: low RoC ⇒ "fewer incentives to target", high RoC ⇒ "more likely to attempt the same attack again" (PDF p. 13; md 586). (ii) Time-series metrics are read as forcing attacker adaptation: "attackers will have to change their strategies: in turn, it needs more money, impacts success rate" (PDF p. 15; md 657). (iii) §3.3 asserts observed attacker disruption in narrative ("forcing fresh scans and adaptation to new flaws", PDF p. 7; md 327) without any figure or metric of attacker behaviour. No attacker-side quantity (time-to-compromise, re-scans, dwell) is plotted; all plotted quantities are graph-derived defence metrics (ASP, AR, AC, RoC, IPV, PSV, NC).

## E. Sensitivity / parameter analysis

Present in substance but never labelled as sensitivity analysis; it is embedded in two results subsections:
- **Network size** — (a) hosts n ∈ {10, 20, 50, 100, 200} for IPV and IPV+PSV, n ∈ {10, 15, 20} for PSV (Fig. 5, PDF p. 12; §4.1 md 572–574); (b) VMs ∈ {50, 100, 200, 400} for R/AC/RoA under six defence conditions (Fig. 8, PDF p. 15; §4.5 md 614–624). One-at-a-time; no grid. Range justification: **not stated** beyond "to comprehend their scalability and performance" (md 624). Claim supported: "smaller networks benefit more from IP-shuffling techniques" (md 574) and "linear growth in all indicators" with scale (md 614).
- **Time** — IPV over 0–140 s (Fig. 5a, c) and AC/AR/RoC/ASP over 0–100 (Fig. 9, PDF p. 16); reported as line charts inside §4.1 and §4.6. Claim supported: time-sensitive MTD outcomes differ from static analysis (md 659).
- Not varied: trigger interval (fixed 10 m, Table 4), number of replicas r, weightings in Algorithm 2/3, CVSS inputs, VM-per-host capacity. No appendix.

## F. Discussion / limitations

Discussion is a **separate section** (§5 "Discussion and future research opportunities", PDF pp. 15–16; md 661–673) with limitations folded in after a series of interpretation paragraphs; there is no "Limitations" or "Threats to validity" heading. Contents, in order:
- Interpretation by mechanism: diversity "introduces irregularity at lower strata, adding intricacy and expanding the time and assets required for an attacker" (md 665); redundancy and shuffling "prevent[] attackers from gaining a stable foothold, compelling them to continuously adapt" (PDF p. 15); SDN "amplifies the effectiveness" via vIPs "without impacting end-user interaction"; IP shuffling "makes it difficult for attackers to maintain an accurate view of the network" (PDF p. 15).
- Limitation framing sentence: "Even though the study offers insightful information, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that might have affected some results. This section describes these limitations and suggests possible directions for future research to ensure the robustness of future studies" (PDF p. 15; md 669).
- Limitation 1 (deployment realism / QoS), verbatim: "For the purpose of experimental evaluation, MTD techniques were simulated to function on every node in the IoT network in this study. However, implementing MTD techniques across all nodes could potentially cause resource strain and service disruptions. ... Unfortunately, integrating this mechanism into our simulation was not possible. Time limitations and insufficient data regarding user profiles and network interactions further complicate our ability to evaluate the effects of MTD techniques on quality of service (QoS). However, by incorporating this functionality into the simulator, we can further investigate the equilibrium between system security and quality of service" (PDF p. 15; md 671).
- Limitation 2 / future method (service-path routing): "Instead of routing requests through every possible service path in the network, an effective method is to route requests only through the shortest service paths ... As such, access latency can be measured by considering only the shortest paths" (PDF pp. 15–16; md 673).
- Conclusion-level concessions (PDF p. 16; md 693): "The research highlights the need for more investigation of MTD techniques across various network configurations, assessment against sophisticated attacks, and a selective deployment approach to minimize resource consumption and service interruptions in real-world scenarios"; "Given the fixed vulnerability inherent in OS and services, we advocate for the utilization of a dynamic model"; "We concentrated solely on assessing Shuffle, Diversity, Redundancy, and their various combinations".

Attacker-realism concession: only the phrase "assessment against sophisticated attacks" as future work (PDF p. 16); no statement that the attacker model is simplified. Comparability caveat: none about numbers; §4.6 compares *features* with Alavizadeh et al. (Table 5) and argues the absence of time in prior work (md 659). No statement on variance, replication, randomness of vIP assignment, or the mismatch between the 10 min trigger and the seconds-scale time axes.

## G. Transferable vs purpose-specific

**Transfers to an honours dissertation evaluating existing MTD mechanisms against a new attacker model on a simulator:**
- A single **parameter table** listing every system parameter with its value, including the defence trigger interval and the initial (pre-MTD) metric values of the network (Table 4, PDF p. 7) — the "declare the starting state" idea.
- A **threat model** subsection stating attacker location, entry, objective and the vulnerability classes in scope (§3.1, PDF p. 5), with vulnerability inputs tabulated with their source (Tables 1–2, NVD/CVE).
- A **bulleted assumptions list** for the network (which hosts can move where, capacity caps, the fixed target) (PDF p. 5).
- An explicit **mutual-exclusion / concurrency rule** between MTD mechanisms and the trigger regime (proactive, periodic, priority queue) stated in setup (PDF p. 5; md 192).
- A **"before vs after" series** on the same axes for the scale-up figure (Fig. 8) and per-path bars (Fig. 7).
- A **scale-up subsection** run separately from the small worked configuration (§4.5) and a **time-series view** per mechanism (Fig. 9) to show that outcomes are dynamic, not one number.
- A discussion that opens with an explicit "acknowledge several limitations" sentence and states what "was not possible" in the simulator and why (PDF p. 15).

**Artefacts of this paper's own purpose/venue:**
- Results organised by *mechanism* with a hand-worked single-VM example per mechanism (path sums for V5) because the contribution is the THARM calculation framework, not a controlled comparison; consequently no baseline in Figs. 6 and 9 and no explicit difference/ratio against the no-MTD state.
- Metrics that are graph/CVSS-derived (ASP, AR, AC, RoC, IPV, PSV, NC) computed deterministically — hence no replications, seeds or variance; a dissertation whose quantity is a stochastic attacker's time-to-compromise cannot inherit this.
- Attacker "modeled after ... cyber kill chain and MITRE ATT&CK" asserted in narrative without parameters or scenarios (PDF p. 7), and attacker incentive inferred from RoC rather than measured (PDF p. 13).
- A "Comparison with the present study" subsection that is a feature-checklist table against prior papers (Table 5) placed *inside* the results section, plus the claim that prior work "ignores time" — a venue-driven novelty argument.
- A §3.3 "validation" paragraph that asserts outcomes in past tense before the results section; the headline claim also appears in §3.6.6 and the abstract before any figure.
- IoT-specific framing sentences repeated at the head of every results subsection (e.g., "In the IoT context, shuffling helps to obscure device configurations", PDF p. 13).
- Mixed time units (10 min trigger vs 0–140 s and 0–100 axes) left unreconciled; low-resolution multi-panel grids (Fig. 9, 12 panels × 8 series).
