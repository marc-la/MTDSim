# bland2020 — evaluation-section anatomy

Source read: `docs/sources/lit_review/original/4_bland2020machine.pdf` (23 pp., Computers & Security 92 (2020) 101738; text extracted per page with pypdf because the PDF renderer is unavailable in this environment, so figure *images* were not viewable — chart-type statements below are drawn from captions and the prose that describes the figures, and are marked as such) cross-checked against `docs/sources/lit_review/4_bland2020machine.md`. Locators are PDF page numbers ("p.N"); md line numbers ("md L") where the md was the clearer copy.

Paper's own research question / purpose (one sentence): to show that a Petri net with players, strategies and costs (PNPSC) can be mapped onto the reinforcement-learning framework and that an ε-greedy learner can improve an attacker's or a defender's strategy (transition-rate settings) over episodes — RQ1 "Can the features of a Petri net model be mapped to the framework of a reinforcement learning algorithm?", RQ2 "Can a reinforcement learning algorithm be implemented in a Petri net simulator in order to learn cyberattack and defense strategies?" with sub-questions 2.1 (attacker) and 2.2 (defender) (p.2). The authors state in §7: "For this research, the goal was to demonstrate that the machine learning method could work in principle." (p.22).

## A. Skeleton of the evaluation portion

Verbatim headings, with page:

- "4. Petri Net Models of Cyberattacks" (p.14) — **setup part 1**: the two nets (XSS = CAPEC 66 per p.2 / "CAPEC 63" per p.15 — the paper is inconsistent; spear phishing = CAPEC 163), their places/transitions/rates tables (Tables 1–16, pp.4–14), the player-controlled transition sets (p.15, p.17).
- "5. Machine Learning in Cyberattack Models" (p.17) — **setup part 2**: reward function, cost function, ε-greedy, episode definition, termination rule, episode count, ε values, seeding of initial reward, defender reward rule (pp.17–21). Algorithms 1–2 (p.22, typeset inside §6).
- "6. Results" (p.21) — **results and discussion together**: one section, no sub-headings; the last two paragraphs (p.22) are interpretive ("There is a balancing act…", "The second interesting result…").
- "7. Future Works" (p.22) — contains the paper's only explicit limitation statements.
- "8. Conclusion" (p.22–23).

Setup and results are **two separate sections** (§4–§5 vs §6), but setup itself is split across two sections (model, then learner). There is no section titled "Experimental setup", "Discussion", "Limitations" or "Sensitivity". The unnumbered paragraph at the top of §6 is the only place the experimental design (500,000 episodes per case; figures truncated to 100,000) is stated as a whole (p.21).

## B. Setup — what is declared before any result

- **Network configuration(s):** not a network in the topological sense. The "environment" is the PNPSC net itself (p.4: "In this work, the environment is the PNPSC net itself, which is a model of a cyberattack."). Two nets, one per CAPEC attack pattern: cross-site scripting (Figs. 2–5, four phases: explore, experiment, exploit, goals) and spear phishing (Figs. 6–9, same four phases) (p.14–15). Size is given implicitly by the place/transition tables: XSS explore 16 places / 14 transitions (Tables 1–2, pp.4,6); experiment 19 / 18 (Tables 3–4, p.7); exploit 23 / 22 (Tables 5–6, pp.7–8); goals 12 / 16 (Tables 7–8, p.10). Spear phishing explore 20 / 14 (Tables 9–10, p.13); experiment 18 / 22 (Tables 11–12, pp.13–14); exploit 11 / 10 (Tables 13–14, p.14); goals 12 / 16 (Tables 15–16, p.14). How chosen: "Although those two patterns were chosen for the current work, PNPSC nets are expected to be able to model most or all of the CAPEC attack patterns" (p.2) — no further selection reason stated. Nets were "validated by a panel of subject matter experts" in a structured face-validation process (p.15, citing Cantrell et al. 2018); the validation criteria are listed (p.15: does CAPEC represent the attack; does the net represent CAPEC; does the net represent the attack).
- **Attacker model / scenarios:** two players, attacker and defender, each defined by (i) the transitions it controls and (ii) the places it can observe (formal 14-tuple, p.3). Attacker-controlled transitions for XSS are listed by name (16 transitions, p.15); defender-controlled for XSS are 13 (p.15); for spear phishing the attacker controls 14 (p.15–17) and the defender only 3 ("cT9, cT10, and dT11 are the only defender-controllable transitions. Due to the nature of a spear phishing attack, the majority of the attempted exploit occurs before a defender can detect it." p.17). Player-controlled transition rates are restricted to {0, 10}: "For this research, we set player-controlled transitions to have rates of either zero or ten. This allowed a player to deactivate a transition, ensuring that it never fires, or activate a transition and give it the opportunity to fire." (p.3). Three experimental **cases** per net, named in the abstract and §6: "a dynamic (learning) attacker against a static (fixed) defender, a static attacker against a dynamic defender, and a dynamic attacker against a dynamic defender" (p.1); in §6 the wording is "static opponents" vs "dynamically compete" (p.22). The cases are not bold-defined or given short labels; they are referred to by description each time. No CAPEC-external attacker properties (skill, dwell, stealth) are declared.
- **Defence conditions:** the defender is a player, not a mechanism. Its controllable transitions "represent the defender attempting to learn that an attack is occurring … The final transition, dT11, represents the defender actively stopping an attack." (p.15). "The defender was allowed to set rates during each phase of the attack." (p.20). Defender reward rule: "The only time the defender is given a positive reward is when the defender chooses to block the attack while place dP9 is marked. All other times, the attacker is assumed to have failed to get to the point where the defender is able to perform an action. We did not count the attacker's lack of ability as a success for the defender." (p.20–21). **No-defence baseline: no.** The nearest analogue is the "static defender" case, in which defender transitions keep their table rates (p.22: "static rates given in Tables 10, 12, 14, and 16").
- **Parameter table: yes**, sixteen of them, but they are model-structure tables, not an experiment-parameter table. Tables 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16 have columns "Transition | Description | Rates" (e.g. p.6, p.7, p.8, p.10, p.13, p.14); Tables 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 have "Place | Description". **Distributions declared: yes** — every transition rate is "the rate parameter λ of an exponential distribution representing the transition's stochastic inter-firing time" and firing is race-based: "the transition with the smallest random variate, i.e., the smallest inter-firing time, is fired" (p.3). **Rate provenance declared as notional:** "The rates used in this work were notional and randomly selected between one and ten. Identifying realistic rates is a future effort to enhance this research." (p.3). The learner's own parameters are declared in prose, not tabulated: success reward = 100, failure = 0 (p.17); cost = rate-change difference scaled by maximum possible difference (p.18; Algorithm 2, p.22: `Cost ← Success(Rate) − Difference(Rate, PreviousRate)·100 / (N_T · N_R)`); ε ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04} (p.20); optimistic initial reward per rate-set = 1,000 (p.20) — note Algorithm 1 (p.22) initialises `R(a) ← 100`, which is inconsistent with the prose's 1,000; recorded here, not resolved.
- **Replications:** "we stop at 500,000 episodes. An episode is a single cyberattack that is simulated to completion." (p.18). "Although the simulation was run 500,000 episodes for each case, the majority of learning occurred in the first 100,000 episodes. Therefore, all resulting figures only highlight the first 100,000 episodes." (p.21). **Termination rule** (per episode): "The simulation ends whenever a success place of the Petri net is marked or whenever there is no longer at least one transition that is able to fire." (p.18); success places named: dP8 attacker, dP10 defender (p.18). Number of independent runs/seeds per case: **not stated** (the 500,000 episodes are one learning run's episodes, not replications). Random-seed handling: not stated. Horizon in simulated time: not stated (episodes are event-driven to absorption).
- **Statistics:** the reported quantity is a **running average reward** per player-observable marking: "The reward for a rate associated with a player-observable marking is a running average of the rewards received for that given rate." (p.18); the plotted series is "a running average based on the reward given at the end of each episode in which place aP2 was at one time marked" (p.21). Point values quoted in prose (28.54, 7.52, 21.54, 9.53, 30.89, 29.06, 28.04, 21.67; pp.21–22). No standard deviation, no confidence interval, no min–max, no significance test, no error bars mentioned anywhere.
- **Anything else declared:** implementation is a bespoke "Petri net simulator" with the RL algorithm inside it (p.2 RQ2; p.22 "implementing the Petri net simulator and the machine learning algorithm"); no language, hardware, run time or version stated. CAPEC version 2.11 (p.14). The learning method is ε-greedy multi-armed-bandit-style averaging per marking, following Sutton & Barto (2018) (p.18, p.20, Algorithm 1 p.22).

## C. Results — how the numbers are shown

- **Order of results:** by attack pattern, then by player, then by opponent type. (1) XSS, dynamic attacker vs static defender (Fig. 10, Table 17); (2) XSS, dynamic defender vs static attacker (Fig. 11, Table 18); (3) XSS, both dynamic (Figs. 12–13); (4) spear phishing, dynamic attacker (Fig. 14, Table 19); (5) spear phishing, dynamic defender (Fig. 15, Table 20); (6) spear phishing, both dynamic (Figs. 16–17) (pp.21–22). The order mirrors the RQ order (2.1 attacker then 2.2 defender) and the model-presentation order (XSS first). The static-opponent cases come first and the dynamic-vs-dynamic case is framed as "An interesting result occurs, when …" (p.22), i.e. as the follow-on.
- **Every figure in the evaluation portion** (captions verbatim; chart type from prose — images not viewable in this environment):
  - Fig. 10 "Attacker Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for cross-site scripting." (p.12). Two panels: "Fig. 10(a) is the overview of the average reward over 100,000 episodes. Fig. 10(b) is a zoomed in portion of Fig. 10(a)." (p.21). x = episode index (to 100,000); y = running average reward; one series (marking aP2); no facets beyond the (a)/(b) zoom.
  - Fig. 11 "Defender Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for cross-site scripting." (p.15). "Figs. 11-13 are formatted similarly." (p.21).
  - Fig. 12 "Attacker Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for cross-site scripting with dynamic defender." (p.16).
  - Fig. 13 "Defender Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for cross-site scripting with dynamic attacker." (p.17).
  - Fig. 14 "Attacker Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for spear phishing." (p.18). Also (a)/(b) overview + zoom (p.22); "Figs. 15–17 are formatted similarly." (p.22).
  - Fig. 15 "Defender Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for spear phishing." (p.19).
  - Fig. 16 "Attacker Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for spear phishing with dynamic defender." (p.20).
  - Fig. 17 "Defender Average Reward over 100,000 episodes for spear phishing with dynamic attacker." (p.21).
  All eight are the same chart: one learning curve per (net × player × opponent-type) cell. The figures float far from the text that cites them (Fig. 10 is on p.12 inside §3 Related Work; §6 begins p.21).
- **Every table in the evaluation portion:**
  - Table 17 "Sample strategy for cross-site scripting attacker determined by the maximum Average Reward." (p.16) — 10 rows × 3 columns (Average | Marking | Rates). Each row is one observed marking, its best average reward, and the rate vector that achieved it.
  - Table 18 "Sample strategy for cross-site scripting defender determined by the maximum Average Reward." (p.19) — 10 rows × 3.
  - Table 19 "Sample strategy for spear phishing attacker determined by the maximum Average Reward." (p.20) — 7 rows × 3.
  - Table 20 "Sample strategy for spear phishing defender determined by the maximum Average Reward." (p.22) — 6 rows × 3.
  These tables compare nothing across conditions; they exhibit the learned policy. The word "Sample" is the paper's own (they are excerpts, not the whole strategy).
- **How the baseline/no-defence condition appears:** there is none. The "static" opponent is the implicit reference and appears only through the prose contrast ("Both the attacker and defender results were generated with static opponents. An interesting result occurs, when the players are allowed to dynamically compete", p.22). The static-opponent value and the dynamic-opponent value are never placed in the same figure or table.
- **How comparisons are stated in prose:** as absolute values with direction words, never as percentages or ratios: "The attacker's average reward drops to 21.54 while the defender's average reward rises to 9.53." (p.22); "The attacker's average reward decreased slightly to an average of 28.04. The defender's average reward had a greater decrease to 21.67." (p.22). Trend statements: "the average reward has a positive trend as shown in Fig. 10" (p.21); "a similar increasing average reward trend is observed" (p.22). One ε comparison in prose only: "The spear phishing attack has an average reward of 30.89 when using an ε of 0.02 … Using an ε value greater than zero shows that a better strategy can be found by exploring." (p.22). Recommendation-form statements appear in the interpretive paragraphs: "A better approach may be to weight the reward based on the episode that it was determined." (p.22). No "A outperforms B" ordering statements between defences, because there is only one defender.

## D. Narrative — the claims, in order

1. Learning happens early: "the majority of learning occurred in the first 100,000 episodes" — used to justify truncating every figure (p.21).
2. XSS attacker: "the average reward has a positive trend … The positive trend implies that the machine learning algorithm is finding or improving the attacker's strategy so as to increase the likelihood of achieving the player's objective." (p.21); aP2 average = 28.54; sample strategy in Table 17 (p.21).
3. XSS defender: "a similar increasing average reward trend is observed"; "When starting the defense, the best option is to activate aT13 and aT14. This leads to an average reward of 7.52. This is without the benefit of including the attacker's inability to succeed in the defender's reward." (p.22).
4. XSS both dynamic: attacker 28.54 → 21.54, defender 7.52 → 9.53 (p.22).
5. Spear phishing attacker: 30.89 at ε = 0.02; "Using an ε value greater than zero shows that a better strategy can be found by exploring." (p.22).
6. Spear phishing defender: 29.06 "from their starting state in the exploit phase C … This phase is the first phase that a defender can perform an action." (p.22).
7. Spear phishing both dynamic: attacker 30.89 → 28.04, defender 29.06 → 21.67; causal attribution: "This is due to the attacker having set rates higher than the static rates given in Tables 10, 12, 14, and 16." (p.22).
8. Interpretive claim on ε: "There is a balancing act that must occur between exploration and exploitation … Too much exploration incurs the cost of changing transitions. A completely greedy selection method may never discover the true optimal action" (p.22).
9. Interpretive claim on learned policies: "several of the transitions are deactivated, versus being activated … This implies that it is better to not attempt to attack or defend in certain scenarios." (p.22).
10. Conclusion restates that the RQs are "clearly" answered (p.22).

**Claims about the ATTACKER's properties from the data:** yes, in two forms. (a) Structural, pre-data: "Due to the nature of a spear phishing attack, the majority of the attempted exploit occurs before a defender can detect it." (p.17) — a property of the net, stated in setup. (b) From the learned policy: the deactivation finding (claim 9) is made about both players ("it is better to not attempt to attack or defend in certain scenarios", p.22), and Tables 17/19 exhibit which attacker actions the learner turns off (e.g. XSS marking (aP10,bP3,cP1) sets cT8 = cT11 = cT14 = 0, Table 17 p.16). (c) The dynamic-vs-dynamic drop in defender reward is attributed to attacker rate-setting (claim 7). No claim is made about attacker speed, dwell, detectability or success probability as a measured quantity.

## E. Sensitivity / parameter analysis

**Present only in a minimal, unreported form.** The one parameter that is explicitly varied is the ε-greedy exploration rate: "Four different values were selected, 0, 0.01, 0.02, and 0.04. The higher the value, the greater the chance for exploration." (p.20). This is **one-at-a-time** (one scalar, four levels). Range justification: none beyond the sentence quoted. **Reporting:** no figure, no table, no per-ε numbers except one — "an average reward of 30.89 when using an ε of 0.02" for spear phishing (p.22); the ε used for the XSS results and for the defender results is not stated. The ε sweep is used to support exactly one claim: "Using an ε value greater than zero shows that a better strategy can be found by exploring." (p.22), plus the qualitative "balancing act" paragraph (p.22). Where it sits: the ε levels are declared in §5 (setup, p.20); the single result is inside §6; there is no sensitivity section or appendix.

What is **not** swept: transition rates (fixed per Tables 2–16 and declared "notional and randomly selected between one and ten", p.3); the player-controlled rate alphabet ({0,10}, p.3); reward magnitudes (100/0, p.17); the optimistic initialisation (1,000, p.20); episode count (fixed at 500,000, p.18). The net is never varied in size or structure; two nets are two attack patterns, not two points on a parameter.

Declared-but-deferred: "Identifying realistic rates is a future effort" (p.3) and "The values for success, cost, and rate variables are all notional and currently have no relation to a real computing system." (p.22) — the paper names the parameters that would need sensitivity treatment without performing it.

How net parameters are **declared** (the question the parent asked about specifically): every transition is enumerated in a three-column table with an integer rate 1–10 (Tables 2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16); the exponential-race semantics are stated once in §2.1 (p.3); the player-modifiable subset and its two allowed values are stated once in §2.1 (p.3) and enumerated by transition id in §4 (p.15, p.17). How they are **swept:** they are not.

## F. Discussion / limitations

- **Discussion is folded into §6 Results** as the final two paragraphs (p.22): the exploration/exploitation "balancing act" and the deactivated-transitions observation. No "Discussion" heading. Threats to validity: none named as such. Comparability caveats: none (no comparison to other work's numbers).
- **Limitations live in "7. Future Works"** (p.22), verbatim:
  - "For this research, the goal was to demonstrate that the machine learning method could work in principle."
  - "A primary area that needs further research is how to apply the models and the machine learning algorithm to a realistic computing environment. The values for success, cost, and rate variables are all notional and currently have no relation to a real computing system."
  - "Obtaining realistic values relies on reports from system administrators and managers who are hesitant to reveal detailed information about their systems especially when considering the impact of an attack."
  - "Another future work is to build a complete example of the computing environment including the details on the decision process to choose the values previously mentioned."
- **Earlier limitation sentences in setup:** "The rates used in this work were notional and randomly selected between one and ten. Identifying realistic rates is a future effort to enhance this research." (p.3).
- **Attacker-realism concessions:** only the notional-rate concession above. The nets themselves are defended as expert-validated (p.15), which is the paper's realism argument.
- **Conclusion-side hedging:** "The methods can be customized to specific computer systems by adjusting the success and cost reward values and the allowable transition rates." (p.23) — the customisation sentence doubles as an acknowledgement that the reported values are placeholders.

## G. Transferable vs purpose-specific

**Transfers to an honours dissertation evaluating existing MTD mechanisms against a new attacker model on a simulator:**
- Enumerating every stochastic transition (or event) with its rate in a table before any result, and declaring the distribution family and the race/firing semantics once (p.3; Tables 2–16). This is the "parameter table" convention in its Petri-net form.
- Declaring rates as notional and saying so at the point of declaration *and* in the limitations (p.3, p.22), rather than only at the end.
- A single unnumbered paragraph at the head of Results that states the whole design (episodes per case; what is plotted; why the plot is truncated) (p.21).
- Naming the termination rule for an episode precisely (absorbing success place, or no enabled transition) (p.18).
- Presenting cases in RQ order and grouping figures identically ("Figs. 11–13 are formatted similarly", p.21) so a reader can compare panels by position.
- Attributing a result to a declared mechanism ("This is due to the attacker having set rates higher than the static rates given in Tables …", p.22) — naming the declared choice that could explain a number.
- Exhibiting a learned/observed attacker policy as a table of (state → action) rows (Tables 17–20) — a way to show *what the attacker did*, not only what score it got.

**Artefacts of this paper's own purpose/venue:**
- Learning curves (running average reward vs episode) as the sole result form; 500,000-episode single runs with no replications, no seeds, no dispersion — appropriate for an "in principle" RL demonstration, not for a comparative evaluation.
- No no-defence baseline and no comparison between defences — there is one defender player, so the comparative axis is static-vs-dynamic opponent.
- Reward-magnitude and ε-greedy design (100/0, 1,000 optimistic init, ε ∈ {0,…,0.04}) — RL-specific.
- The structured face validation by an expert panel (p.15) as the realism argument — tied to the CAPEC-to-Petri-net modelling programme, not to simulator evaluation.
- Sixteen place/transition tables occupying ~10 of 23 pages — model documentation for a formalism paper.
- Sensitivity essentially absent; the ε sweep is declared but not reported — a convention *not* to copy.
