## (i) Sec. VIII — Evaluation methods for MTD (L679–L731; p22–p24)

### Framing
- Four method classes, in order: "(1) analytical models; (2) simulation models; (3) emulation models; and (4) real testbeds." (L681; p22)
- Analytical is split into VIII-A-1 probabilistic models and VIII-A-2 graphical security models (GSM/HARM) (L685, L693; p22).

### VIII-A-1 Probabilistic models (L685–L691; p22)
What the surveyed studies varied (the survey's own description of *parameters* in analytic MTD studies):
- Carroll et al. [25]: ASP "with respect to the network size, the address space scanned, the degree of system vulnerability, and the frequency of shuffling operations." (L687; p22)
- Crouse et al. [41]: ASP "while varying the network size, the size of honeypots deployment, and the number of vulnerable nodes." (L689; p22)
- Cho and Ben-Asher [32]: SPN model, integrated defence "compared to the system with various combinations of defense mechanisms (i.e., an IDS only or IDS plus either deception or MTD) in terms of ASP and MTTSF (i.e., system lifetime)." (L689; p22) — i.e. a factorial of defence *conditions* rather than parameters.
- Okhravi et al. [124]: MTTSF; Zhuang et al. [183]: "frequency of diversity-based MTD and ASP"; Sharma et al. [147]: ASP and defence cost; Luo et al. [108]: ASP for port hopping (L687–L689; p22).
- Pros and cons, verbatim (L691; p22): "The evaluation of probabilistic models provides insights and lessons based on the observations of general system behaviors with minimum evaluation cost. However, due to the probabilistic parameterization of all the key system design features, a certain level of simplicity and abstraction is unavoidable and may not be able to capture some deviations or unexpected effects that can be introduced in real application scenarios."

### VIII-A-2 Graphical security models (L693, L704; p22–p23)
- HARM: two layers, AG upper (reachability) and AT lower (per-node vulnerabilities) (L693; p22). Hong et al. [77] "capture dynamic changes in the network resulting from deployed MTD techniques throughout the duration of the simulation" via temporal GSM + "dynamic security metrics" (L693; p22).
- Pros and cons, verbatim (L704; p23): "The main advantages of using GSMs are the ease of evaluation and representation. Furthermore, GSMs can be adopted to compute various security metrics based on the MTD application. A GSM can be easily visualized and helps the network administrators to find out the vulnerabilities of the network and choose appropriate defensive strategies like MTD techniques. However, generating and analyzing GSMs in largescale networks suffers from a scalability issue. Although this has been improved by using HARM which is more scalable than the other GSMs, it is still challenging to model the very large-scale networks using GSMs."

### VIII-B Simulation models (L706–L698 — md places the "Pros and Cons" paragraph at L698 before the heading at L706 due to column ordering; p23)
- Prevalence claim, verbatim (L708; p23): "Most studies evaluating the performance of MTD approaches have been validated based on simulation experiments."
- What simulation buys over analytics, verbatim (L708; p23): "Compared to analytical models, simulation models have more flexibility in modeling specific attack behaviors (e.g., periodic attacks [91], DDoS attacks [183], Multi-Armed Bandit policy-based attacks [130], sequential attacks on attack surface [28]), and various testing scenarios characterizing unique environmental features (e.g., cloud-based service computing [129], wireless sensor networks using IPv6-based MTD [174]). Some simulation studies add MTD features into existing network simulators (e.g., NeSSi2 [183])." (continuation at L696)
- **The sensitivity-analysis statement** (the only explicit use of the phrase in the paper), verbatim (L698; p23): "Simulation models provide high flexibility in modeling diverse types of attacks, environmental conditions, and different types of MTD techniques as most design features can be easily parameterized without much restriction. Due to the flexibility and experimental capability to conduct sensitivity analysis by varying the values of key design parameters, it allows us to easily obtain meaningful insights before performing the implementation in a real system. However, the simulation model has its inherent limitations because all possible variables may not be captured due to the nature of existing, uncontrollable uncertainty. Therefore, the lessons obtained from the simulation studies may not be realized in real testbed-based experiments or systems."
- Related earlier mention of a simulation study's varied factors (Sec. IV-B-4, L324): Zhuang et al. [183] "examined how the network size, the frequency of shuffling/adaptations, and the number of vulnerable computers affect the effectiveness of the MTD technique based on the experimental results tested from the network security simulator, NeSSi2."

### VIII-C Emulation models (L700, L710–L712; p23)
- "results from emulation testbed-based experiments can provide an even higher validity on experimental results although emulationbased studies are not as common as simulation studies." (L710; p23)
- Examples all Mininet-based ([15], [16], [87], [152]); metrics named: "success rate and overhead, which measures defensive costs, such as storage cost and end-toend (ETE) delay" [16]; "time-to-first-byte and total download time" and "security robustness is evaluated by testing several classes of cyberattacks" [152] (L710; p23).
- Pros and cons, verbatim (L712; p23): "Emulation models can provide a higher validity than simulation models in the experimental results validated through them. In addition, they also provide higher flexibility in modeling realistic attack types and MTD techniques than simulation-based evaluation. However, most emulationbased test environments (e.g., Mininet) running on a single machine share the same hardware resources for all emulation elements, making it difficult to evaluate experiments for large-scale networks."

### VIII-D Real testbeds (L714–L729; p23–p24)
- SDN testbeds [110], [75], [165]; cloud/web [7], [93], [163], [130] (L716–L727; p24).
- On [7] (Alavizadeh): "deploying MTD techniques on the real cloud and obtaining the real measurements can be a difficult process since the cloud providers have their own policies and restrictions. Thus, the real measurements presented in this work may be used for further researches." (L727; p24)
- Pros and cons, verbatim (L729; p24): "Real testbeds can help verify the performance of MTD techniques under more realistic system environments. However, it is difficult to evaluate the MTD techniques in large-scale networks. Most real testbed-based studies have focused on assessing the performance of MTD in small or mid-sized networks."

### Table VI — "EVALUATION METHODS OF MTD TECHNIQUES: PROS VS. CONS" (p24, journal p732), transcribed verbatim from the rendered page

| Evaluation Method | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Analytical Models | Providing theoretical background and understanding with minimum cost; ease of modeling, representation, and evaluation | Scalability issues with some analytical models (e.g., GSM, SPN); limitations in reflecting realistic scenarios due to abstraction/simplicity traded off for efficiency |
| Simulation | Better flexibility in attack/system modeling than analytical models; easy parameterization for sensitivity analysis | Limitations due to inherent uncertainty toward real-world applications |
| Emulation | High validity; high modeling flexibility; more realistic scenarios | Lack of scalability due to issues in limited hardware computational resources; still maintaining a certain level of abstraction/simplicity |
| Real Testbeds | Highest flexibility and validity in modeling and experiments among all other available evaluation tools | Least scalability in complexity and highest cost among all available evaluation tools |

Framing sentence: "We summarize the pros and cons of each evaluation method discussed above in Table VI." (L731; p24)

### Other evaluation-method statements outside Sec. VIII
- Sec. IX-A enterprise networks (L752): "As seen in Section VIII, most MTD approaches have used simulation-based experiments for the performance validation. Similar trends are observed in evaluating MTD techniques for enterprise networks, which are mainly validated based on simulation models [22], [36], [52], [54], [91], [168], [173] while the use of a model-based probability/analytical model was not common [32]."
- Sec. IX-C CPS (L789): Li et al. [105] "conducted a simulation study ... using a synthetic CPS network traffic and real-world network traces".
- Sec. IX-E cloud (L827): Bardas et al. [20] "analyzed security benefits to verify its performance with an e-commerce scenario".
- Sec. IX-F vehicular (L865): Wu et al. [169] "conducted their experiments on a real CAN testbed to verify security enhancements in terms of the information entropy".

## (ii) Attacker-model critique (Sec. V-D) and the metric catalogue (Sec. VII, Figs 6–8, Fig 16)

### Sec. V-A Characteristics of advanced attacks (L375–L398; p14–p15)
Four attacker characteristics the survey says MTD work assumes: **Persistent** ("not performing a onetime attack (e.g., APT attacks ...)"; multi-stage, outside→inside), **Adaptive** ("wisely manage their resource limits and at the same time opportunistically seek to compromise an entire system"), **Stealthy** ("stay stealthy until the time comes"), **Incentive-driven** ("a rational actor that is sensitive to incentives, such as attack success with minimum cost [52]") (L379–L396). "As a typical type of sophisticated attacks, the Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) has been commonly considered as an attack type that techniques need to countermeasure" (L398).

### Sec. V-B Cyber kill chain (L402–L423; p15)
Seven CKC phases listed [82] (L406–L418). MIT Lincoln Lab five-phase variant noted (L420). "Limitations of Considering APT Attacks:" verbatim (L421; p14): "Existing MTD approaches heavily consider attackers in the stage of reconnaissance when the attackers are outside attackers. However, in many cases, stealthy, undetected inside attackers are more serious threats to the system when they are not properly handled, such as detecting them by an IDS. But considering a high volume of false detection, MTD can significantly help intrusion detection by adding another layer of defense against the inside attackers."

### Sec. V-C Attack types and Fig. 5 (L425–L452; p14–p15)
- Count basis: "We summarized the main attack types considered in existing MTD approaches with 36 papers published for 2010-2018 in Fig. 5. Note that more than one technique or attack can be considered in each work." (L445; p14)
- "The three major attacks the existing MTD techniques have mainly addressed are reconnaissance attacks, DoS attacks, and APT attacks." (L445; p14)
- Fig. 5 (bar chart, p15; y = "Number of MTD studies for 2010-2018", x = attack type, series = Shuffling / Diversity / Redundancy), values read from the rendered figure: Side-channel 0/1/0; Web-related 0/2/1; Worm 3/0/0; Buffer-overflow 4/0/0; Reconnaissance 10/2/2; DoS 12/0/0; APT 5/5/0.
- APT definition used (L437): "aiming to access system resources, control them, and perform exfiltration attacks ... by performing stealthy, persistent, and adaptive attacks ... APT attacks are also called 'multistage attacks' that refer to attackers performing multiple attacks across multiple stages."

### Sec. V-D Limitations of the current attack models (L454–L465; p15) — the attacker-model critique, three bullets, verbatim heads
1. **"Smart, intelligent attackers are significantly less considered:"** "If attackers are intelligent such that they are able to detect defense patterns by using learning mechanisms ... then they can easily capture what types of MTD techniques are used and what the patterns are to trigger an MTD operation. However, highly intelligent, learning attackers are not really considered. Generally, it is assumed that attackers have certain attack patterns, rather than that they learn and can launch adaptive attacks. Whereas the contrary is true for defenders; they are often permitted to take proactive, adaptive defense actions based on an assumed highly intelligent learning capability. This assumption may not be true; even the attackers are often smarter than the defenders in practice [83]." (L458)
2. **"Few scenarios are considered to deal with multiple strategies by attackers and defenders:"** "most MTD approaches focus on a single or a small set of attacks, and few scenarios have considered multiple strategies that can be taken by attackers or defenders. For example, the attacker may need to decide which system vulnerability it needs to target while the defender may want to determine which defense mechanism needs to be used, where both parties aim to choose a cost-effective decision under resource-constrained, timesensitive settings." (L460)
3. **"An attacker has been less considered as a rational decision maker with learning ability:"** "The smart attackers can leverage how MTD works and what side-effect the MTD can introduce. In addition, they can capture the defense patterns for an adaptive MTD which aims to realize a cost-effective MTD. However, attackers with learning capability and their mental models have been rarely studied while high intelligence with the defender and corresponding intelligent MTD (e.g., machine learning-based MTD) is easily assumed and has been proposed [38], [158], [163], [180]." (L462–L465)

### Sec. VII Metrics — the purpose axis and the perspective axis (L566–L677; p19–p22)
- Opening claim: "no standard metrics have been proposed to measure their effectiveness and efficiency." (L568; p19)
- The catalogue is organised on TWO axes: purpose = **effectiveness** (VII-A) vs **efficiency** (VII-B); perspective = **attacker's metrics** vs **defender's metrics** inside each. Stated: "The attacker's metric estimates its attack performance, indicating that the attacker's high performance refers to the defender's low performance, and vice-versa. The defender's metric measures its performance in achieving security and defense goals of a given system." (L574)

**VII-A Effectiveness — attacker's metrics (L576–L590):** Attack success probability (ASP) [12 refs] incl. Rahman's "attackability"; Attack utility; Learning by attackers; Mean time to compromise a system (MTTC) — "how long an attacker takes to compromise an entire system ... similar to mean time to failure (MTTF)"; Unpredictability; Attack surface.

**VII-A Effectiveness — defender's metrics (L592–L630):** Defense success probability (DSP); MTTF ("the same as MTTC under the attacker's metrics", L596); Defense utility; Learning by defenders; System security — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Degree of vulnerability; Other metrics — Controllability, Worm propagation speed, Vastness, Periodicity, Uniqueness, Revocability, Distinguishability, Loss in rewards between optimal and executed deployment.

**Fig. 6** (pie, p21; "Metrics measuring MTD effectiveness by an attacker's perspective"), counts read from the figure: ASP 12, Attack utility 7, MTTC 5, Unpredictability 3, Attack surface 2, Learning 1.
**Fig. 7** (pie, p21; "Metrics measuring MTD effectiveness by a defender's perspective"), counts: Others 17, System security 14, Defense utility 5, MTTF 4, DSP 4, Learning 1.
- Count basis: "summarized in Figs. 6 and 7 based on 27 papers published during 2011-2018. Note that more than one metric can appear in a single paper." (L636; p21)
- Trends stated (L636; p21): "(1) the attack success probability ... is a dominant metric used for the effectiveness of MTD aiming to minimize this metric; (2) due to a large volume of game theoretic approaches ... the payoff or utility of attackers or defenders is also one of the dominant metrics ...; and (3) some system-level metrics measuring system vulnerability or reliability (e.g., degree of vulnerability, MTTF or mean time to compromise an entire system) in the presence of attacks are observed as major metrics used to measure MTD effectiveness."

**VII-B Efficiency — attacker's metrics (L640–L646):** Penalty in attack payoff ("cost is 1 for attacking; 0 otherwise"); Attack cost (e.g. Nmap scanning overhead [93]). "Due to the similar nature of both metrics and their small volume, we omit the figure." (L646)

**VII-B Efficiency — defender's metrics (L648–L673):** QoS to users; System performance (message overhead, operational delay/cost, dropped connections, throughput); Defense cost (abstract migration/maintenance cost; infrastructure level; Address space overhead; Flow table size; Integrated performance cost); Strategy switching cost; Power consumption.
**Fig. 8** (pie, p22; "Metrics measuring MTD efficiency by a defender's perspective"), counts: System performance 13, Defense cost 12, QoS to users 3. Basis: "25 papers published during 2011-2018" (L675; p22).
- Trend + prescription (L677; p22): "most metrics measuring MTD efficiency belong to system performance or defense cost. However, the level of QoS provided to users is significantly less studied. ... Since deploying and executing MTD mechanisms to a system introduces a critical trade-off between security, defense cost, and service availability, an optimization problem with these dual conflicting goals should be investigated in-depth to meet multiple criteria from both system goals (i.e., multi-objective optimization problem) [31]."

**Fig. 16** (concentric wheel, p31; "Security, performability, and economical MTD metrics"): three rings around "METRICS in MTD": SECURITY (Return on Attack, Attack Success Probability, Attack Cost, System Risk, Other Metrics), PERFORMABILITY (Reliability, Performance, Availability, Other Metrics), ECONOMICAL (Operational Cost (Opex), Capital Cost (Capex), Other Metrics); outer labels Shuffling / Diversity / Redundancy / Combinations of MTD Techniques. Prescription in XI-B (L928–L929; p31): "Security metrics can measure the effectiveness of the MTD while performability metrics can estimate the efficiency of the MTD. Moreover, the economical costs introduced by using the MTD should be considered, particularly when MTD is implemented in real systems. Hence, finding critical tradeoffs between these three metrics should be investigated in the evaluation stage."

## (iii) How many experiments / network sizes / attacker scenarios the surveyed studies used

- Corpus sizes the survey itself counts: 36 papers (attack types, 2010–2018, L445); 27 papers (effectiveness metrics, 2011–2018, L636); 25 papers (efficiency metrics, 2011–2018, L675). Table I compares 6 prior surveys (L88).
- Number of experiments per surveyed study: **not stated** anywhere.
- Network sizes: no numbers given for any surveyed study. Qualitative only: real testbeds "focused on assessing the performance of MTD in small or mid-sized networks" (L729; p24); GSMs "still challenging to model the very large-scale networks" (L704); emulation "difficult to evaluate experiments for large-scale networks" (L712). Network size is named as a *varied factor* in Carroll [25] (L687), Crouse [41] (L689), Zhuang [183] (L324) — ranges not stated.
- Attacker scenarios in surveyed studies: named per method class — simulation: "periodic attacks [91], DDoS attacks [183], Multi-Armed Bandit policy-based attacks [130], sequential attacks on attack surface [28]" (L708); emulation: "several classes of cyberattacks" [152] (L710); real testbed: "Web bots" [163], "Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) policy-based attacks" [130] (L727). Enterprise-domain attacks: "worm attacks [54], DDoS [54], [91], [168], abstracted attacks in an attack-defense game [52], scanning attacks [22], [32], [36], APT attacks [173], or more sophisticated, multi-stage attacks, including circumvention attacks, deputy attacks, entropy reducing attacks, probing attacks, and incremental attacks [49]." (p24 col 2, Sec. IX-A "Main Attacks")
- Count of attacker scenarios per study: **not stated**; the survey's own verdict is "most MTD approaches focus on a single or a small set of attacks" (L460).
- Replications/seeds/CIs in surveyed studies: **not stated**.

## (iv) Lessons learned / insights about evaluation design

### Sec. X Limitations (p30; md L868–L881 — see note at top)
- "**Limited investigations on the interplay between MTD and other defense mechanisms**" (L881; p30) — truncated in the source.
- "**Few studies investigating the effect of MTD on reducing attacks beyond the reconnaissance stage:** Most attack behaviors considered in the existing MTD works occur during the reconnaissance stage. This implies that MTD mainly deals with outside attackers as its primary goal is to protect a system before they break into the system. This significantly limits the applicability of MTD techniques although MTD can enhance system security and performance by dealing with attackers beyond the reconnaissance stage (e.g., inside attackers)." (L869; p30)
- "**Lack of investigating the optimal deployment of multiple, hybrid MTD techniques**" (L871; p30).
- "**Metrics used in the existing MTD approaches have limitations in measuring multiple dimensions of a system's quality:** The state-of-the-art MTD metrics mostly only measure the effectiveness in terms of system security. A few studies have tried to assess performability aspects of the system but left out economical aspects (e.g., operational and capital cost)." (L873; p30)
- "**Lack of realistic testbeds:** Most MTD approaches have been validated based on analytical or simulation models while emulation-based or real testbed-based evaluation methods are rarely used. This indicates a critical need for developing better experimental testbeds for verification and validation (V&V) to test the performance of diverse types of MTD techniques." (L875; p30)

### Sec. XI-A Insights & Lessons Learned (L887–L908; p30–p31), the two evaluation-relevant bullets verbatim
- "**Validation of the performance of MTD with diverse metrics:** The effectiveness and efficiency of MTD are measured by using a variety of metrics in terms of the perspectives of both an attacker and a defender." (L904)
- "**Effective and efficient evaluation methods developed to ensure the verification and validation (V&V) of MTD:** Most MTD approaches have been validated based on simulation or analytical model-based experiments. But some existing approaches conducted experiments under more realistic environments using emulation or real-testbed based evaluation methods." (L906)
- Also: "**Importance of balancing multiple objectives:** ... The thorough analysis of critical tradeoffs between multiple objectives is necessary to improve the state-of-the-art MTD technology." (L898)

### Sec. XI-B Future Research Directions, evaluation-relevant bullets (L910–L929; p31)
- "**More useful metrics are needed to maintain service availability to users:** ... most metrics are focused on measuring system security or attack success. Since the system has multiple objectives to meet in terms of security, defense cost, and service availability, there should be a balance to develop meaningful, useful metrics that can capture the impact introduced to users in terms of service quality." (L926)
- "**The effectiveness and efficiency of MTD should be measured by system metrics embracing security, performability, and economical costs introduced by the MTD**" (L928; see Fig. 16 above).
- "**More adaptive MTD mechanisms need to be developed:** ... How to learn an attacker's action or learn system security conditions is vital for the defender to make decisions for the optimal MTD deployment." (L918–L922)

### Sec. II-D / VI-D discussion caveats that bear on evaluation
- Game-theoretic modelling caveats (L558): "we should keep the caveats in mind in terms of the rationality of players, solution complexity, and misperception or uncertainty in decision making".

## Summary of the prescriptive content (record, not evaluation)
1. Simulation is the dominant MTD evaluation method (L708; L752; L906) and its distinctive affordance is "easy parameterization for sensitivity analysis" by "varying the values of key design parameters" (Table VI p24; L698). Its stated cost is that "the lessons obtained from the simulation studies may not be realized in real testbed-based experiments" (L698).
2. Metrics are to be reported on an effectiveness/efficiency purpose axis crossed with an attacker/defender perspective axis (L572–L677); the survey's forward prescription is a three-way security / performability / economical split with tradeoffs examined "in the evaluation stage" (L928; Fig. 16 p31).
3. The attacker-model critique is that surveyed attackers are pattern-following, non-learning, single-strategy and reconnaissance-stage, while defenders are granted learning and adaptivity (L458–L465; L421; L869).
4. Parameters the survey names as varied in prior analytic/simulation MTD studies: network size, address space scanned, degree of system vulnerability / number of vulnerable nodes, shuffling frequency, honeypot deployment size, diversity (L324; L687; L689). No ranges, replication counts or statistics are recorded for any surveyed study.
