\begin{abstract}
Moving Target Defence (MTD) continuously shifts a system's attack
surface to invalidate an attacker's reconnaissance and raise the cost
of intrusion. Modern networks, however, are themselves dynamic---cloud,
software-defined, and IoT deployments change topology, configuration,
and workload at runtime---and these changes interact with, and can
undermine, the effectiveness of MTD. This dissertation investigates how
the inputs and realism of an AI-driven MTD (AI-MTD) module affect
defensive outcomes in a simulated network. It contributes (i) a more
capable adversarial model grounded in MITRE ATT\&CK campaigns and cyber
threat intelligence, and (ii) an exploration of integrating an
Intrusion Detection System (IDS) as an additional signal for the MTD
agent. \emph{[Replace this abstract with your final results once the
evaluation is complete.]}
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Background and Motivation}
Traditional cyber defence is reactive: vulnerabilities are found and
patched after the fact. Moving Target Defence (MTD) inverts this by
continuously reconfiguring the system so that the information an attacker
gathers becomes stale before it can be exploited~\citep{jajodia2011mtd}.
The difficulty addressed in this work is that contemporary networks are
themselves \emph{dynamic}: topology, configuration and workload change
at runtime, and these changes alter how effective a given MTD technique
is.

\section{Problem Statement}
This project studies how the input parameters, system realism, and
attacker sophistication of an AI-driven MTD (AI-MTD) module affect
defensive outcomes in a simulated network environment.

\section{Research Aims and Questions}
The project investigates three aims: (1) evaluate the effectiveness of
MTD techniques as changes are introduced into the network; (2) measure
how effectiveness varies for different types of change; and (3) develop
adaptive MTD configuration that minimises the disruption caused by
dynamic change.

\section{Contributions}
This dissertation contributes: a more capable adversarial module built
from MITRE ATT\&CK campaigns and cyber threat intelligence
(Chapter~\ref{ch:design}); and a feasibility study of integrating an
Intrusion Detection System signal into the AI-MTD agent
(Chapter~\ref{ch:impl}).

\section{Dissertation Structure}
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews MTD, dynamic networks and threat
modelling. Chapter~\ref{ch:design} presents the system and threat model
and the design of the attack and IDS modules. Chapter~\ref{ch:impl}
describes the implementation. Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} reports the
evaluation, Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses the findings, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Tactic-Level Adversarial Profiling}
\label{sec:tactic-profiling}

This work inherits its simulation environment from earlier MTD
evaluation studies \citep{brown2023,zhang2023}; the simulated network
itself is described in Section~\ref{sec:system-model}. The attacker
that environment provides is procedural: a six-phase scan-and-exploit
loop that prices progress by reachability and CVSS-scaled exploitation
and races toward a single target. That attacker is retained unchanged
as the baseline, since preserving it keeps this work comparable with
the studies it extends. It cannot, however, carry the evaluation
alone.
Because the baseline attacker is fast and pursues no larger objective,
an MTD technique that merely out-paces it wins by default, and a
finding that one mechanism outperforms another may reflect the
attacker's simplicity rather than the defence's quality. The evaluation
therefore needs a second attacker: one that is slower, pursues an
explicit campaign objective, and is grounded in observed adversary
behaviour.

This section designs that attacker. It profiles adversary behaviour at
the granularity of the MITRE ATT\&CK tactic
(Chapter~\ref{ch:background}), assigning each of the fifteen enterprise
tactics two properties: a \emph{duration}, expressing how long the
tactic occupies the attacker (Section~\ref{sec:tp-duration}), and a
\emph{reset classification}, expressing whether a defensive mutation
(a single MTD action, such as an address shuffle or a service-version
change) invalidates the tactic's gains (Section~\ref{sec:tp-reset}). Section~\ref{sec:tp-profiles} then
derives both properties for each tactic from the behavioural
literature, and Section~\ref{sec:tp-synthesis} draws out the pattern
the fifteen profiles form and the limits of what they can claim.

\subsection{Choice of Modelling Granularity}
\label{sec:tp-granularity}

Adversary behaviour could be modelled at three granularities. The
finest, the individual ATT\&CK technique, is where genuinely empirical
timing exists: \citet{ling2023} derive time-to-compromise estimates for
techniques from vulnerability data. Their method, however, requires
real vulnerability instances to attach to, and this simulator's
vulnerabilities are synthetic (Section~\ref{sec:tp-scope});
maintaining justified timings for hundreds of techniques is also
impractical. The coarsest granularity, the kill-chain phase, can be
grounded in campaign reporting but cannot express the distinctions the
MTD interaction turns on: credential reuse versus fresh exploitation
within the same phase, or patient dwell versus a fast action. The
ATT\&CK tactic sits between the two: coarse enough that the sparse
public threat-intelligence corpus can populate behaviour at that
granularity \citep{rodriguez2024}, fine enough to separate behaviours
that a mutation treats differently. Precedent stops short of what is
needed here: the one tactic-level ATT\&CK Petri-net model in the
literature is untimed \citep{rodriguez2024}, and timed models carrying
ATT\&CK labels either attach timing at the technique level via CVE data
\citep{ling2023} or declare transition rates without per-tactic
justification \citep{xiong2021,bland2020}. The per-tactic duration and
reset assignments below are therefore constructed from the behavioural
literature rather than imported from an existing catalogue.

\subsection{Profile Construction and Scope}
\label{sec:tp-scope}

Each campaign objective evaluated in this study has its own profile,
aggregated from analyst-drawn attack flows in public threat
intelligence. Aggregation makes a profile a behavioural
\emph{envelope}, a space of plausible campaign behaviour for that
objective, rather than a replay of any single documented intrusion,
and it over-generates by construction: a simulated campaign may combine
technique choices that no one real campaign combined. This is accepted
and disclosed rather than corrected. Throughout this thesis,
\emph{advanced persistent threat} (APT) describes the genre of campaign
the grounding intelligence documents: slow, objective-driven, and
accumulating footholds and credentials as it goes
\citep{alshamrani2019,chemat2024}. The term claims nothing about
reproducing the adaptive behaviour of any named group.

Four boundaries fix what the model does not attempt. First, the
simulator's vulnerabilities are synthetic, so techniques cannot be
bound to real CVE instances; behaviour is modelled at the ATT\&CK layer
only. Second, the simulator implements shuffle- and diversity-class MTD
but no redundancy-class mechanism, so no claim about redundancy is
made. Third, the simulator models no intrusion detection: in this
study an attacker is ``caught'' only in the sense that a mutation
invalidated its progress, never in the sense that a sensor observed
it. This boundary has particular consequences for the stealth profile
(Section~\ref{sec:tp-t-stealth}). Fourth, the modelled attacker does
not adapt: it neither recognises that a mutation has occurred nor
learns the mutation schedule. Within these boundaries the model's claim
is deliberately modest: not that the profiled attacker is \emph{true},
but that behavioural fidelity at this level changes the answers an MTD
evaluation returns.

\subsection{Duration Model}
\label{sec:tp-duration}

The simulator natively prices two attacker actions: a network scan,
which takes a fixed duration, and an exploit, whose duration scales
with the complexity of the targeted vulnerability
\citep{brown2023,zhang2023}. No other tactic has a native price, and
Section~\ref{sec:tp-granularity} established that no empirical
catalogue of per-tactic durations exists to import. The duration model
therefore works by anchoring. Tactics are collected into five timing
groups; each group takes one anchor duration; and each tactic's
duration is a relative multiplier applied to its group's anchor
(Table~\ref{tab:tp-groups}). Fixing four non-zero anchors rather than
fifteen independent durations also keeps the calibration problem
identifiable: the literature can support a handful of anchors, not
fifteen free parameters.

Because the values differ greatly in how well they can be grounded,
each carries one of three validation tiers, and the tier states exactly
how much trust the value claims. A \textbf{Tier~1} duration is the
simulator's own: the scan and exploit anchors are inherited unchanged,
and tactics priced by them are not tuned. A \textbf{Tier~2} duration is
calibrated: breach reporting states campaign-level milestones (for
example, the time from initial access to exfiltration), and the
objective-execution anchor is adjusted until the emergent campaign
timeline reproduces the reported pattern. A \textbf{Tier~3} duration is
declared: no observable grounds it, so it is set by a written argument
and examined across a \emph{sweep band}, a stated range over which the
evaluation's sensitivity analysis varies the value, rather than trusted
at a point.

Calibration is only possible under a scale convention. The simulator
prices actions in simulated seconds, while the observables in breach
reporting run from hours to months. Reproducing absolute real-world
timelines would stretch the simulated campaign until stealth dwell
dominated every other term, and time-to-compromise would no longer be
comparable with the baseline attacker's. The literature is therefore
used for \emph{relative} structure (orderings and ratios between
tactics), while absolute scale stays anchored to the simulator's
native values. This follows the operational-validation strategy for
models whose micro-parameters cannot be measured directly
\citep{sargent,grimm}: free parameters are judged by whether the
model's observable output reproduces known patterns. The calibrated
claim here is that the emergent campaign timeline reproduces the
reported \emph{shape} of real campaigns, never their absolute length.

A worked example makes the scheme concrete. Persistence has no native
simulator action, so its duration is Tier~3. The APT literature places
its character at the slow, patient pole but supplies no number, so it
is assigned to the stealth-anchored group at the reference multiplier
($\times 1.0$); and because the value is declared rather than measured,
the evaluation sweeps it across $\times 0.25$--$\times 4$.
Reconnaissance, by contrast, is Tier~1: the simulator already meters it
as a scan, and its duration is the scan anchor, untouched.

\subsection{Reset Classification}
\label{sec:tp-reset}

The simulator already encodes a rule for what a defensive mutation does
to an attacker: it invalidates the attacker's position and knowledge
gains and forces re-work, but compromised hosts and harvested
credentials are retained \citep{brown2023,zhang2023}. A mutation is a
partial, layered reset, never a clean wipe. The profiles apply this
rule tactic by tactic, and the central observation is that the outcome
is decided by the \emph{kind of gain} a tactic produces, not by the
tactic as a whole. The same address shuffle that erases a scanning
attacker's map of the network does nothing to a stolen credential,
because a credential is not bound to any location; a tactic that
produces both kinds of gain is split within itself. Each profile in
Section~\ref{sec:tp-profiles} classifies its tactic's gains into four
classes:

\begin{description}
  \item[\textsc{vulnerable}] the gain is position or knowledge; an
    address or topology mutation invalidates it and forces re-work.
  \item[\textsc{survivor}] the gain is a capability (a foothold, a
    credential, an elevated token) that is not location-bound; a
    mutation leaves it intact.
  \item[\textsc{partial}] the gain is contested rather than cleanly
    resolved: a re-planning penalty, a channel built to survive
    disruption, or a rate contest with no fixed outcome.
  \item[\textsc{null}] the gain is held off-network, outside the
    terrain any mutation touches.
\end{description}

The simulator's mechanics fix each classification's direction; the open
quantity is magnitude. How much of a tactic's progress one mutation
destroys is expressed as a per-tactic \emph{reset fraction}, and no
public dataset measures it. The fractions are therefore declared and
swept across bands bounded by the mechanism arguments in each profile,
which is the established practice in timed attack and MTD modelling
\citep{mcqueen2006,bland2020,xiong2021}. Where the literature argues a
direction the simulator cannot yet exercise, the profile records the
divergence rather than resolving it silently; Section~\ref{sec:tp-synthesis}
collects those cases.

\subsubsection{Reconnaissance (TA0043)}
\label{sec:tp-t-recon}

Reconnaissance builds the attacker's external map: which hosts are
reachable, which ports are open, and what services and operating
systems they run. The APT literature characterises it as patient and
largely non-exploitative (port and service scanning, registry
queries, fingerprinting) \citep{alshamrani2019}, and
\citet{jalowski2026} observe that attackers facing a mutating network
may study the mutation pattern itself. The simulator meters
reconnaissance as its native scan action, so the tactic takes the scan
anchor at $\times 1.0$ (Tier~1); the slower, externally staged tempo of
real reconnaissance is a recorded divergence in shape, not a separate
duration. The tactic's product is pure position knowledge, so it is
classified \textsc{vulnerable}: an address or topology mutation
invalidates the map and forces a re-scan. Scan-disruption studies bound
the magnitude: even an idealised shuffle only reduces the success of a
complete scan pass to roughly 63\% when vulnerable hosts are sparse,
and the achieved disruption rises and falls with the ratio of mutation
rate to scan rate \citep{carroll2014}.
One boundary tempers the classification: the simulator never mutates
exposed perimeter endpoints, so reconnaissance of the route in
survives; it is the interior map that resets. The model does not
capture an attacker who times its scans around the mutation schedule,
so the modelled reset is a worst case for a naive scanner.

\subsubsection{Resource Development (TA0042)}
\label{sec:tp-t-resdev}

Resource development covers the infrastructure, accounts, and tooling
an adversary assembles before an intrusion. Its techniques are staged
off the victim network \citep{alshamrani2019,selmanaj2024}, so however
long it takes in reality, it precedes the simulated clock: the modelled
adversary arrives already equipped. It is assigned to the off-network
group with a zero in-simulation duration (a Tier~3 modelling
decision rather than a measurement), and its reset classification is
\textsc{null}: its gains are held outside the terrain any mutation
touches. Its role in the model is to mark where the MTD interaction
begins.

\subsubsection{Initial Access (TA0001)}
\label{sec:tp-t-initaccess}

Initial access is the transition from off-network preparation to a
foothold. Its server-side form, exploitation of a public-facing
application, is exactly the action the simulator's complexity-scaled
exploit duration prices, so the tactic takes the exploit anchor at
$\times 1.0$ (Tier~1). Its social-engineering forms (phishing, watering
holes) involve a delivery-then-wait pattern in which the attacker
waits for a user to trigger execution \citep{alshamrani2019}; the
simulator does not meter that wait, and this is recorded as a known
divergence rather than a separate duration. The reset classification
splits in time. A \emph{completed} foothold is a capability gain and
\textsc{survives}: the compromised host stays compromised, and because
perimeter endpoints are never mutated, the route in is permanent. An
\emph{in-progress} exploit attempt is surface-dependent, and a
diversity mutation that changes the target's software version
mid-attempt can defeat it \citep{evans2011}, so the attempt is
classified \textsc{partial}. An entry made with a valid stolen account
is unaffected by either mutation family.

\subsubsection{Execution (TA0002)}
\label{sec:tp-t-execution}

Execution, the running of attacker code on a host, is mechanically
brief and underlies most other tactics. The APT literature associates it with
concealment, particularly fileless, in-memory execution
\citep{alshamrani2019}, but the slowness this implies belongs to the
spacing between actions rather than to the act itself. The profile
therefore leans to the fast reading, at $\times 0.5$ of the stealth
anchor, with the widest relative band among the enabling tactics
($\times 0.1$--$\times 2$), because neither the simulator nor the
intelligence corpus pins the value (Tier~3). The reset classification
is \textsc{partial} and depends on the mode of execution: in
\citeauthor{evans2011}'s analysis of attack classes, diversity defences
offer no advantage against execution that abuses legitimate
functionality (the mode APTs prefer) and disrupt only
fresh-exploit execution, and then only at high re-randomisation rates
\citep{evans2011}. The simulator does not distinguish execution modes,
so in practice execution inherits the reset behaviour of the tactic it
serves.

\subsubsection{Persistence (TA0003)}
\label{sec:tp-t-persistence}

Persistence is the durability layer of a campaign: backdoors, implanted
accounts, and boot-level mechanisms that let access survive the
ordinary churn of a live environment. The literature places its dwell
at the months scale and describes its mechanisms as difficult to evict
\citep{alshamrani2019,cho2020}. No simulator action prices
``maintain a foothold'', so persistence takes the stealth anchor at
$\times 1.0$ as a declared value (Tier~3) with a wide band
($\times 0.25$--$\times 4$). Its reset classification is the most
contested in the catalogue. The simple reading is \textsc{survivor},
since a compromised host stays owned. \citet{flipit2013}, however,
model foothold control as a rate contest in which whichever side moves
faster and more cheaply controls the resource, so whether periodic
mutation evicts an entrenched foothold has no fixed answer. The
simulator implements no eviction or reimaging operation and therefore
realises the survivor extreme of that contest; the swept reset fraction
stands in for the contest the simulator cannot yet run, and the
classification is recorded as \textsc{partial}, a contested survivor,
rather than resolved.

\subsubsection{Privilege Escalation (TA0004)}
\label{sec:tp-t-privesc}

Privilege escalation produces an elevated-permission state that gates
which techniques are available to the attacker, formalised by
\citet{xiong2021} as a permission level an adversary must raise. Its
canonical form is local vulnerability exploitation
\citep{alshamrani2019}, priced by the exploit anchor at $\times 1.0$
(Tier~1); its other form, reuse of a stolen token or valid account, is
a fast variant within the same group. The reset classification is
bimodal in the same way as initial access: an elevated token, once
held, is not bound to a network position and \textsc{survives}, while
an in-progress escalation exploit is a surface-dependent attempt that a
diversity mutation can defeat. \citet{maleki2016} show that the
probability of defeating an MTD grows with the time and cost an
attacker can spend, which bounds what an interrupted attempt loses. The
permission gate itself is a precondition tracked by the attacker model
rather than a simulator state, so in practice the escalation's reset is
the reset of its underlying exploit or credential action.

\subsubsection{Stealth (TA0005)}
\label{sec:tp-t-stealth}

Stealth, the concealment half of the former Defense Evasion tactic,
gives the low-and-slow campaign its name
\citep{alshamrani2019,cho2020}. Concealment is less an action with a
duration than a tempo imposed on every other action, so its modelled
duration represents the spacing it adds, taken as the stealth-group
reference at $\times 1.0$ (Tier~3). Of all the declared values, this
one leans hardest on the shape-not-scale convention of
Section~\ref{sec:tp-duration}, because absolute stealth dwell is the
least observable quantity in the model. The reset classification is
dominated by a structural boundary from Section~\ref{sec:tp-scope}:
the simulator models no detection, so there is no sensor to hide from,
and concealment has no direct representation beyond spacing. The
literature grounds an MTD effect on stealthy attackers as a
re-planning penalty, in which a mutation forces the attacker to re-plan
paths that avoid observation, and the classification is
\textsc{partial} on that basis. What the simulator computes for this tactic is therefore
a proxy, and the substantive MTD-versus-stealth contest lies in a
detection regime outside this study's scope.

\subsubsection{Defense Impairment (TA0112)}
\label{sec:tp-t-defimpair}

Defense impairment is the disabling half of the former Defense Evasion
tactic: terminating endpoint protection, stopping backups, and similar
acts. Two uncertainties compound here. Behaviourally, the APT
literature indicates adversaries prefer to avoid defences rather than
disable them \citep{alshamrani2019}, and when disabling does occur it
is a brief, decisive act immediately before a noisy payload; the
profile accordingly leans fast, at $\times 0.5$ of the stealth anchor,
with the widest band in the catalogue ($\times 0.1$--$\times 4$). The
value is Tier~3: the intelligence corpus reports this tactic's
prevalence, not its duration. For reset, a disabled control is host-local software
state: an address or topology mutation re-addresses the host but leaves
the killed control killed, so the classification is \textsc{survivor},
invalidated only by reprovisioning the host. The simulator, however,
models no defensive-control state at all, so this classification is
conceptual --- an argued direction rather than a computed one
(Section~\ref{sec:tp-synthesis}).

\subsubsection{Credential Access (TA0006)}
\label{sec:tp-t-credaccess}

Credential access is the heart of the post-foothold campaign;
credential dumping is reported as the most common precursor to lateral
movement \citep{alshamrani2019}. Dumping is on-host work the simulator
can price, so the tactic takes the exploit anchor at $\times 1.0$
(Tier~1). Its reset classification is the clearest \textsc{survivor} in
the catalogue. A harvested credential is a standing capability rather
than a position on the map: an address mutation changes where hosts
are, not whether a stolen secret still authenticates, and a diversity
mutation changes software versions, not the secret's validity. The one
defence family that does bite, credential rotation, is not among
the mechanisms this study evaluates, and is imperfect in any case:
\citet{zhangMonroseReiter2010} found that a replacement password could
be derived from its captured predecessor for about 41\% of accounts
under offline attack and 17\% under online attack. The behavioural
consequence matters to the whole evaluation: the baseline attacker
reuses credentials only opportunistically, whereas a credential-led
campaign concentrates its progress in exactly the modality that surface
mutation cannot reset.

\subsubsection{Discovery (TA0007)}
\label{sec:tp-t-discovery}

Discovery is reconnaissance run from inside a foothold: the same host,
service, and network enumeration, taking the same scan anchor at
$\times 1.0$ (Tier~1). Unlike lateral movement, its gain has no capability component at all
(an internal map is pure position knowledge), which makes discovery
the cleanest \textsc{vulnerable} tactic in the catalogue. An address or topology mutation invalidates
the map wholesale and forces re-enumeration; in one published
discrete-event experiment, a sufficiently short mutation interval drove
a scan-first agent's success probability to zero because every mutation
forced it to restart its scan \citep{reti2022}.
The effect scales with the ratio of mutation interval to scan cadence,
and the more complete the map, the more one mutation destroys. It is
the address and topology family of mutations that bites here;
service-version diversity is largely orthogonal, since discovery
enumerates reachability and structure rather than exploitable versions.
The model does not capture strategic re-discovery --- an attacker who
re-enumerates selectively around a known schedule --- so, as with
reconnaissance, the modelled reset is a naive-attacker worst case.

\subsubsection{Lateral Movement (TA0008)}
\label{sec:tp-t-lateral}

Lateral movement extends the campaign from host to host, ranging in
character from a patient manual pivot to an automated worm-style sweep
\citep{alshamrani2019}. Its dominant form, remote-service login or
exploitation, is priced by the exploit anchor at $\times 1.0$
(Tier~1). Its reset classification splits on how a hop finds and
reaches its target, and the split is the sharpest illustration of the
per-modality principle of Section~\ref{sec:tp-reset}. A
\emph{scan-based} hop builds a target map and is \textsc{vulnerable}:
in address-mutation experiments against scanning worms, the scanner is
left with under 1\% valid targets
\citep{alshaer2012_rhm,jafarian2012_ofrhm}.
A \emph{credential-based} hop (pass-the-hash, valid accounts) rides a
surviving capability, and the same mutation that defeats the
scanning worm does not touch it: \textsc{survivor}. Which modality
dominates is set by the campaign objective, so a credential-led profile
is close to reset-immune in this tactic while a scan-led one is highly
disruptable. The model does not capture an attacker who recognises a
post-mutation state collision and re-routes around it.

\subsubsection{Collection (TA0009)}
\label{sec:tp-t-collection}

Collection is where a campaign begins to act on its purpose, gathering
and staging data before theft \citep{alshamrani2019}. It belongs to the
objective-execution group at $\times 1.0$ (Tier~2): incident reporting
characterises the collect-then-exfiltrate chain well enough to
calibrate against, and reports a floor beneath these actions: they depend on human activity
and data throughput and cannot be made arbitrarily fast
\citep{sophos_aar2025}. The reset classification splits on where the
data lives. Data staged on
a compromised host is a held capability and \textsc{survives} mutation,
exactly as a harvested credential does. A read from a remote share
depends on reachability, so a topology or address mutation can sever
the route mid-collection: \textsc{vulnerable}. The simulator has no
first-class ``collected data'' object, so the surviving half of this
classification is carried by the attacker model over foothold
durability rather than computed from simulator state.

\subsubsection{Command and Control (TA0011)}
\label{sec:tp-t-c2}

Command and control (C2) is a long-lived, low-signal channel maintained
from shortly after the foothold until the campaign ends, typically
beaconing on a configurable cadence \citep{alshamrani2019}. Beaconing
over common ports exploits no vulnerability, which is why CVE-based
timing methods cannot price it \citep{ling2023}; the tactic takes the
stealth anchor at $\times 1.0$ (Tier~3). The rate that matters to MTD
is not the channel's lifetime but its beacon interval, which is what a
mutation interval competes against. The classification is
\textsc{partial}, for a reason peculiar to this tactic: real channels
are architected to survive disruption, with fallback channels, proxy
and CDN fronting, and domain-generation algorithms existing precisely
so that losing one route does not sever control \citep{selmanaj2024}.
The simulator models a simpler, single-route channel, so a modelled
mutation disrupts C2 more than the literature suggests it should --- a
divergence that runs in the opposite direction from most in this
catalogue, harsher on the attacker than reality. Campaigns that do not
depend on a live channel at all \citep{chemat2024} sidestep the contest
entirely.

\subsubsection{Exfiltration (TA0010)}
\label{sec:tp-t-exfil}

Exfiltration is the terminal act of a data-theft campaign. It joins the
objective-execution group at $\times 1.0$ (Tier~2); the time from
initial access to exfiltration is one of the few milestones breach
reporting states directly, and it is held out as a validation
observable rather than used for fitting
(Section~\ref{sec:tp-duration}). Its duration band is wide, spanning a
bulk transfer at one end and a deliberately fragmented trickle at the
other. Its reset behaviour is inherited from command and control: a
transfer in progress rides the C2 route, so a mutation that disturbs
the route interrupts the batch --- a \textsc{partial} reset --- while
the staged data and the external destination are untouched.
Attacker tradecraft blunts the effect further: careful campaigns split
exfiltration into batches sent to servers at different addresses
\citep{alshamrani2019}, so one mutation costs at most one batch. That
batching is behaviour the attacker model must encode; it is not a
simulator primitive.

\subsubsection{Impact (TA0040)}
\label{sec:tp-t-impact}

Impact spends the campaign's accumulated access, and its duration is
the most objective-dependent in the catalogue: a ransomware actor's
impact is a throughput-bound burst of minutes to hours, while an
espionage campaign may never execute impact at all
\citep{alshamrani2019,alsada2024}. It takes the objective-execution
anchor at $\times 1.0$ (Tier~2), with the ``never'' end expressed
structurally: profiles whose objective does not include impact omit the
tactic, rather than assigning it a zero duration. Impact is also the
one tactic where MTD acts by limiting blast radius rather than by
invalidating gains: no mutation un-encrypts a host, but mutation can
restrict how many further hosts the impact reaches:
\citet{barach2026} report a runtime-surface MTD holding ransomware
encryption to roughly 13\% of hosts by blocking lateral spread. The
classification therefore splits on reachability: the act on a held host
\textsc{survives}; the spread to new hosts is \textsc{vulnerable}. The
simulator does not model destruction as an irreversible state change,
so the surviving half is conceptual (Section~\ref{sec:tp-synthesis}).
Objective conditioning is at its sharpest here: the tactic is inert for
espionage profiles and decisive for ransomware ones.

\subsection{Synthesis and Limitations}
\label{sec:tp-synthesis}

Across the catalogue, the classifications organise into one pattern.
The gains that are network position (the reconnaissance and discovery
maps, the scan-based lateral hop, the remote read in collection, the
spread of impact) are exactly what surface mutation invalidates. The
gains that are capability (a foothold, a credential, an elevated
token, staged data, a disabled control) survive the same mutation. The remainder sit between: contested by rate
(persistence), architected to survive disruption (command and control,
exfiltration), or reachable only through a proxy in this simulator
(stealth, execution). The pattern carries a consequence for defence:
the gains a campaign accumulates once inside the network are
predominantly of the surviving kind, so an MTD tuned to spoil
reconnaissance and exploitation addresses the part of the kill chain
the intruder has already left behind.

Not all the classifications rest on the same footing, and the
difference matters for interpreting the evaluation. Most are exercised directly by
the simulator: it already resets network position and already retains
compromised hosts and harvested credentials, so the \textsc{vulnerable}
classifications and the credential and foothold \textsc{survivor}
classifications inherit their direction from what the code does. Four
are instead conceptual: Defense Impairment (the simulator models no
defensive-control state), the local half of Collection (no collected-data
object), the irreversibility of Impact (no destructive state change),
and the eviction extreme of Persistence (no reimaging operation). For
those four, the classification is an argued hypothesis about the sign
of a reset fraction, not something the simulator demonstrates, and each
is flagged as such in its profile.

Two limitations frame what this section can claim. First, the profiled
attacker differs from the baseline through two families of introduced
parameters: the per-tactic durations and the per-tactic reset
fractions. Declaring such rates and sweeping them is the established
practice in timed attack and MTD modelling
\citep{mcqueen2006,bland2020,xiong2021}, and the bands used here are
bounded by mechanism arguments rather than left free, but the
parameters remain declared rather than measured. Second, the claim that
behavioural fidelity changes an evaluation's answer is only meaningful
if the MTD ranking produced by the profiled attacker is stable across
the swept bands and differs from the ranking produced by the baseline
attacker. Both are questions for the evaluation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}), which tests exactly that: ranking stability
across the uncertainty bands, and divergence between the two attackers'
rankings. Until those experiments run, the profiles constitute a
defended design rather than a demonstrated result.
