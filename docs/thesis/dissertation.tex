We made the attack graph using Attack Flow. Attack Flow is maintained
by the Center for Threat-Informed Defense (CTID), which sits under
MITRE Engenuity \citep{ctid2025attackflow}. This is a project that
documents the per-incident behaviour of an APT attacker --- done by
adding sequence and dependency to APTs' known tradecraft. Attack Flow
maintains a documented attack-behaviour corpus spanning 2017 to 2024.
Other approaches were considered to produce this corpus for our
pipeline. Co-occurrence mining gave low coverage and keyword
mining poor fidelity (Appendix~\ref{app:cooccurrence}).
% CONFIRM: the appendix entries pin the numbers (the 80% confidence
% threshold, the single-digit edge counts) --- both entries owed.
NLP-based approaches we ruled out as a class: we judged the relatively
low fidelity of the threat intelligence they produce to be something
we could not defend over the life of the project
\citep{rahman2025, buchel2025}. The strength of using Attack Flow's
corpus is that a human analyst drew every dependency, so there is a
degree of fidelity that other approaches could not provide.

Instead of using each incident independently of the
others --- where there is little insight in the corpus --- we
aggregated the 38 usable attack flows
(Appendix~\ref{app:full-graph}). We
added the graphs together:
we stripped out all of the unnecessary details, such as specific attack
procedures or human analyst notes, preserving the logical AND/OR
structure. But aggregation makes the logical AND
vacuous, because there are many OR paths which an attacker agent
can traverse. Once we had decided on Attack Flow as the preferred mode
of CTI, we then had to decide what the correct resolution was (tactic
or technique). That is why we decided to prioritise coverage at this
stage, whereas in earlier stages fidelity was a large concern. We
leaned towards tactic, because 88\% of the technique-to-technique
directed flows came from a single incident. But once aggregated,
the tactic-to-tactic directed flows had much greater coverage, and we
judged this trade-off between coverage and fidelity was necessary to
capture the generalised behaviour of APT attackers. There is also
precedent for using tactics as the node granularity
\citep{rodriguez2024}.

The aggregate APT attack graph (L1) is a
weighted directed graph where the nodes are the MITRE ATT\&CK tactics,
and each edge is weighted on how many times it is observed in the
corpus. We are using
human-analyst-drawn dependency graphs, and this is sparse input
data. The edge weight is a recurrence value; therefore, we cannot rely
on these weights alone for attacker agent traversal. Instead, we treat
the L1 attack graph as a skeleton of behaviour.
% (Cycles-preserved sentence cut at pass 5 --- L3 now introduces loop
%  preservation itself; recorded in the handoff for the L3 drafting.)

Another limitation we encountered was an issue with a lack of
pre-intrusion dependencies: any tactics before initial access were
sparse compared to the rest of the corpus. Pre-intrusion activity
typically happens on the attacker's infrastructure --- infrastructure
setup, passive recon --- and is not detectable on the defender's side.
Hence, in the corpus, reconnaissance only has a mention in 10 out of
the 38 attack flows. But since we are producing an attack model for
defender evaluation, we will later have to draw these flows in.
This is persistent across CTI, so we
would have to address it regardless of our input.

\subsection{L2: Objective-conditioned attack profiles}
\label{subsec:attack-profiles}

% ---- Pass 5b, 2026-08-17: Marc's further cuts applied --- the "idea to
% ---- capture" opener, the numbers clause, the second concession, the

The literature tells us that APT attackers are not a homogeneous group,
and so they are distinguished by campaign-level intent. We cannot
produce an APT attack model that could be considered behavioural,
because the L1 aggregate carries so many different motivations and
objectives which influence behaviour \citep{alshamrani2019}.

Motivation is something that we cannot ascertain. We can see what an
APT group did; this is an analyst inference.
Although motivation is a taxonomy built into STIX and
well defined, it is left unpopulated in the ATT\&CK STIX data set, in
ATT\&CK groups and in Attack Flow. Objective then becomes what the
attacker has been observed to have done, and this is evident through
the attack flows that the analysts drew.

We are extrapolating the literature and saying the prefix is invariant,
so we are going to treat the prefix of attackers as invariant and we are
going to look at the terminal tactic \citep{alshamrani2019}. Attack Flow
is composite data, with structure and dependency manually drawn to a
high level of fidelity; however, coverage is an issue. Attack Flow also
uses and operationalises the MITRE ATT\&CK framework, but it does not
limit itself to its taxonomy, and so the attack flows can have nodes
which do not directly map to the MITRE framework and which the prior levels
in the pipeline removed. Some of the attack flows could be left
incomplete, and that is why we had to cross-reference the objective,
because we could not rely on the objective tactic alone.
% [3b, standing] "we are going to" x2; citation here and in P1; "removed".

Hence, we settled on a four-way partition based on the shape of our
corpus. Terminal tactic is the primary evidence for objective, but we
cross-checked against the CTID description, the ATT\&CK page and
vendor reports. That is why 19 of the 38 attack flows land in a
different category from the one defined by the terminal tactic alone
(see Appendix~\ref{app:classification-audit}). There are four: exfiltration objective (19); impact
objective (7); double extortion, which we define as containing both
impact and exfiltration in the same attack flow (7); and no realised
objective (5). We have judged this partitioning
the right balance for corpus coverage: the smallest attack profile has a
minimum of five flows, which provides enough coverage. Six other
partitions were considered and dismissed
(see Appendix~\ref{app:rejected-partitions}).

\subsection{L3: Generalised stochastic Petri-net formalism}
\label{subsec:petri-formalism}

We needed a data structure to pipe in these attack profiles from L2 as
an input, and this is what the Petri nets provide.
% [RULED 2026-08-18, do not re-flag] the 37% number stays out (not needed);
%   "declared and kept apart from the observed profiles" stays out

We ruled out attack graphs, which we already have from L1, because they
do not supply the timing and stochastic elements we needed; DAGs,
because they are acyclic (the L1
attack graph is cyclic). The other forms of Petri net: an SPN does
not have the right mechanism, since it times every transition, and a
DSPN is over-engineered, adding a fixed delay we do not need, so we used
the one in the middle, a GSPN, which provides the immediate transitions
we are using. There is also a large
precedent for stochastic Petri nets in the MTD evaluation
space~\citep{cai2016, chobenasher2018, mendonca2023}.

\subsubsection{The controller layer}
\label{subsubsec:controller}

The controller layer is the join between the Petri nets and our simulator, and
it carries three things: the dwell times (the stochastic dwell times), the
tactic-to-verb mapping, and the failure matrix.

The first is the dwell times. Each tactic consumes some time in
the simulator, as the previous attack phases did from the action layer. But we
cannot derive the dwell times from anything we have done in
the movement layer itself. The dwell times do not exist in the literature; they
do not exist in CTI vendor reports. Prior papers that have done similar work
describe this as inherently arbitrary: trying to put times on an
attack~\citep{bland2020, mcqueen2006, mendonca2023}.

There is no MITRE tactic mapping to time resource. How

MTDSim. What we could take from the six original verbs of the action layer,

If that was not sufficient, then we used some multiplier of those values to
produce the tactic dwell times, and if not, we decided what shape fitted

defender's perspective this is off-network and untrackable. Some tactics map to
the same verb, but in reality the tactics, we would assume, do not take the same
amount of time --- there might be more effort, more time, more resources
required --- so different tactics that map to the same verb still provide a
different amount of time.

The ceiling of this thesis lies in the action layer that we are adopting from
the simulator: the attacker model that we produce can only be as good
as what we adopt. But it is extendable and modular: our movement layer can be
mapped to anything given an appropriate mapping, or a stronger underlying
attack model can be built that operationalises all of MITRE's tactics, and
that would produce a more useful attacker model. We did not have the time to do

The base transition weights are recurrence values from the earlier levels, and
we assume the observed out-transitions are the complete set of choices open to
the token --- an explicit assumption, since the Attack Flow corpus is all we
have to draw on, and a different corpus would give different transitions and
weightings. But a recurrence value does not reflect an attacker, because an
attacker can succeed or fail, and these reports only capture success --- there
is survivorship bias. These values are necessary, but they are not sufficient,
so we have to supplement them with re-weighting to consider if the
attacker agent fails.

One concern was trying to overfit the tactic-to-tactic failure matrix
to the baseline attacker's action layer. This is a

naturally encodes success, so the failures are what is not encoded, and that is
what we are trying to encode here. We needed a 15-by-14 tactic-to-tactic

independently decided: each tactic-to-tactic transition was placed into a given
shape (rules A to I in Figure~\ref{fig:failure-weight-decomposition}). The second is distance: we used the
literature and overlaid the attack
phases~\citep{hutchins2011, alshamrani2019, mandiant2013, chemat2024}, which
gave the four broad phases of an attack --- preparation, intrusion,
post-intrusion operations, and objective. We assigned a distance value: a
transition within the same phase, or one phase away, is not penalised; two
phases away keeps a quarter of its weight; and the maximum distance is weighted
zero. There is a floor of 0.1: if a distance value falls below this threshold,
it is read as exactly zero at fold-in. It makes no sense for an attacker to go
from reconnaissance directly to impact in the MITRE framework, because they are multiple
steps away from each other, as the literature would say. This can be found in
Appendix~\ref{app:weight-sets}, along with the full set of tactic-to-tactic
failure weights.

\subsubsection{Mechanics and the join to MTDSim}
\label{subsubsec:mechanics-join}

We made all of the actions in MTDSim independent of each other, and produced
the join through the controller layer, so that the movement layer supplies all
the tactic timing of the attacker agent at runtime. The loop runs as follows: the
token selects the next tactic; the tactic has a dwell time and an exponential
draw, which is fed into the discrete-event simulator and consumed as time;
the mapping resolves the tactic to a verb (or to dwell-only); once the time is
consumed, a success or failure signal returns, on failure the failure matrix
re-weights the token's out-transitions, and the next tactic is selected
(Figure~\ref{fig:movement-dataflow}).
% [rephrase applied: your dictated loop spec compressed to one runtime
%   sentence + the figure ref; the action-carve moved here from the preamble.]

This is verdict-conditioned re-weighting. The base proportion on all of the
edges is multiplied by the declared failure matrix and renormalised. On success the
token routes on the base proportions, since the corpus already encodes success;
on failure the declared failure matrix applies, producing a different set of
options for the token to decide from. This is what encodes direction.
% [rephrase applied: the one-arm seam fixed --- success = route on the base
%   proportions (v4_failure_only); "encodes direction" once. The retired
%   success matrix is not mentioned, per your ruling.]

Success or failure at runtime comes from the action layer and is propagated
up.
An action fails if the preconditions of the action itself were missing --- for
example, trying to exploit a vulnerability without having scanned for it:
objects are passed around in the simulator, and if the verbs have not been run,
it fails. The same holds for the moving target defence: if MTD interrupts the
attacker, then whatever verb it is on fails. But if they are on a dwell-only,
there is no verdict, so this is not felt by the token in the Petri net.

We employed a sink-retrace policy, because the token would
frequently hit sinks in the Petri net and terminate the run --- a concession to
the thinness of the corpus; we retrace the edge travelled.
Another characteristic is that the simulator we are inheriting largely models the
confusion and disruption of the attacker through a 20-second confusion penalty
that hits the attacker at certain phases. How the defender thwarts the attacker
is inherently tied to the simulator; it is an invariant of the simulator, so we
maintain that 20-second confusion penalty. Not all the timing is supplied by
the Petri net: the confusion penalty is the exception.
